A one-dimensional area law and a matrix product state approximation from a global spectral gap: the collar-free route

Abstract

We take the two September 2026 manuscripts A two-dimensional area law from a global spectral gap and Polynomial PEPS approximation of gapped square-grid ground states and run their arguments in one dimension. Two things happen. First, the area-law proof collapses: the marginal tail bound and the buffered conditional-entropy discount of , together with the Araki–Lieb inequality, already prove \(S(X)\le C(q,R)\,(1+J/\Delta)\) for every interval \(X\). The sub-volume recursion, the positive quasi-local constraints, the replica scanner, the amplification and the two-family geometry of are not needed. The reason is geometric: a shell around an interval has a bounded number of crossing interactions, so the discount costs a constant instead of a boundary-sized error, and the buffer whose entropy must be controlled is a constant number of sites. Second, the tensor-network theorem survives as a linear sweep instead of a dyadic hierarchy. The encoded holes, adaptive patches, angular information estimates and row-column routing of disappear; what remains are the Uhlmann-type birth and exchange moves, the information reset by a Gaussian filter, the dimension-free density compression and the column-extraction lemma. The result is a matrix product state of bond dimension \(CL^{c}\) with vector error \(L^{-1}\). We also record that in one dimension the tail bound makes the matrix product state theorem a two-line corollary of the area law by sequential Schmidt truncation. Every statement in this note is written twice: once in words, once precisely. The area-law proof uses none of the techniques of Hastings or of Arad–Kitaev–Landau–Vazirani; the tensor-network proof uses a Lieb–Robinson bound in exactly one lemma, and we indicate how to avoid it at the price of a quasi-polynomial bond dimension.

Overview

In words. Both source papers are long because two dimensions is hard. The entanglement of a region is produced along its boundary, and in two dimensions the boundary of a region of linear size \(r\) has \(r\) sites: every estimate that costs “a boundary” costs \(r\), and every buffer that one puts around a region to isolate it is itself a region whose entanglement must be controlled. The whole replica, scanner and amplification apparatus exists to break this circularity. In one dimension the boundary of an interval is two points. An estimate that costs a boundary costs a constant, and a buffer of constant width has, trivially, constant entropy. So the circularity never arises, and one short section of the two-dimensional paper proves the whole area law. This is the content of Part .

The tensor-network theorem is a different story. It does not follow from entropy bounds alone (the papers explain why: an entropy bound on each cut does not give a global vector approximation). Its two-dimensional proof distributes the ground vector among “parties” attached to dyadic squares, by local changes of ownership whose errors are controlled by information estimates, then compresses the resulting distributed density into a tensor network. In one dimension a dyadic hierarchy is the wrong geometry: a binary tree drawn on a line has cutwidth \(\log L\), which turns polynomial bond dimension into quasi-polynomial. The right geometry is a sweep from left to right with a moving “remainder” party. All the genuinely one-dimensional difficulties (what to do at the ends of a band when a third owner is adjacent) are solved by exactly the seven-move word trick that the two-dimensional paper uses along an edge. This is Part . We also show that, in one dimension, the tail bound of Part  makes the tensor-network theorem a corollary of the area law by the classical sequential-truncation argument; the sweep protocol is the faithful one-dimensional analogue of the two-dimensional construction, and it is what generalizes.

Precisely. Throughout, \(q\ge 2\) and \(R\ge 1\) are integers and \(J,\Delta>0\). A finite set \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb Z\) carries the induced path graph (edges between consecutive integers both in \(\Lambda\)) and graph distance \(d_\Lambda\). Each site carries \(\mathbb C^q\) and \(\mathcal H_\Lambda=\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}\mathbb C^q\). An admissible Hamiltonian is \[\tag{1} H=\sum_{\substack{\varnothing\ne X\subseteq\Lambda\\ \operatorname{diam}_{d_\Lambda}X\le R}}h_X, \qquad h_X=h_X^*,\qquad \lVert h_X\rVert\le J,\] one summand per support, zero summands allowed. The standing spectral hypothesis is that \(H\) has a unique normalized ground vector \(\Omega\) (up to phase), ground energy \(E_0\), and \[\tag{2} H-E_0\mathrm{I}\ \ge\ \Delta\bigl(\mathrm{I}-|\Omega\rangle\langle \Omega|\bigr).\] For \(A\subseteq\Lambda\), \(\rho_A=\operatorname{Tr}_{\Lambda\setminus A}|\Omega\rangle\langle \Omega|\), \(S(A)=S_\Omega(A)=-\operatorname{Tr}\rho_A\log\rho_A\) with natural logarithms and \(0\log 0=0\), \(S(A|B)=S(AB)-S(B)\) and \(I(A:B)=S(A)+S(B)-S(AB)\) for disjoint \(A,B\). The edge boundary is \(\partial_\Lambda A=\{\{x,x+1\}\subseteq\Lambda:\ \text{exactly one of }x,x+1\text{ lies in }A\}\). Constants \(C,c\) depend only on \(q,R,J,\Delta\) unless stated otherwise and may change from line to line; where a dependence is tracked it is said so. The two theorems of the note are the following.

Theorem 1.1 (One-dimensional area law). There is \(C_{\mathrm{AL}}=C_{\mathrm{AL}}(q,R,J,\Delta)\), with \[C_{\mathrm{AL}}\le C_0(q,R)\,\Bigl(1+\frac J\Delta\Bigr),\] such that for every finite \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb Z\), every admissible Hamiltonian satisfying (2), and every \(A\subseteq\Lambda\), \[S_\Omega(A)\le C_{\mathrm{AL}}\,\lvert \partial_\Lambda A\rvert.\]

Theorem 1.2 (One-dimensional matrix product state approximation). Fix \(q,R,J,\Delta\). There are \(C,c>0\) depending only on these parameters such that for every \(L\ge2\), every admissible Hamiltonian on \(\Lambda=\{1,\dots,L\}\) satisfying [eq:gap], there is a nonzero matrix product state \(\Phi\) on \(\Lambda\) with open boundary conditions and bond dimension at most \(CL^{c}\) such that \[\min_{\theta\in\mathbb R}\Bigl\|\frac{\Phi}{\lVert \Phi\rVert}-\mathrm e^{i\theta}\Omega\Bigr\|\le L^{-1}.\]

The constants never depend on \(\Lambda\), on \(A\), or on the particular Hamiltonian. No gap for a restricted Hamiltonian, no frustration freeness, no translation invariance is assumed.

The one-dimensional area law

Setting, reductions, and counting

In words. Three small reductions make the rest clean. If the domain \(\Lambda\) has several components, the Hamiltonian is a sum over components (every support has finite graph diameter, so it lies in one component), the unique ground vector is a product over components, and entropy and edge boundary both add over components. So we may take \(\Lambda\) to be an interval. A subset \(A\) of an interval is a union of maximal subintervals, each of which contributes at least one boundary edge, and subadditivity reduces the theorem to intervals. Finally, the only two counting facts we need are these: an interval is cut at two points at most, and an interaction of diameter at most \(R\) that crosses a cut lives in a window of \(2R\) sites around that cut. So the number of interactions crossing the boundary of an interval, and the logarithm of their joint dimension, are bounded by constants depending only on \(R\) and \(q\). This is the entire “area” input of the one-dimensional proof.

Precisely. For an interval \(X\subseteq\Lambda\) and a support \(Y\) with \(\operatorname{diam}_{d_\Lambda}Y\le R\), say that \(Y\) is split by \(X\) if \(Y\cap X\ne\varnothing\) and \(Y\setminus X\ne\varnothing\). Since \(Y\) is contained in \(R+1\) consecutive integers and contains a point on each side of one of the two cut edges of \(X\), it lies in one of the two windows of \(2R\) consecutive sites centered at a cut edge. Hence \[\tag{3} \#\{Y:\ Y\text{ split by }X\}\le 2\cdot 2^{2R}=:N_R, \qquad \dim\mathcal H_Y\le q^{R+1},\qquad \ell_R:=\log(\mathrm eq^{R+1}),\] and a term \(h_Y\) split by \(X\) can be expanded by matrix units on \(Y\cap X\) as \[\tag{4} h_Y=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_Y}c_\alpha\otimes d_\alpha,\qquad N_Y\le q^{2(R+1)},\qquad \lVert c_\alpha\rVert\,\lVert d_\alpha\rVert\le J,\] with \(c_\alpha\) supported in \(X\) and \(d_\alpha\) in \(\Lambda\setminus X\). (Each matrix unit \(E_{ab}\) on \(\mathcal H_{Y\cap X}\) has norm one, and the coefficient operator \(d_\alpha=\operatorname{Tr}_{Y\cap X}[(E_{ba}\otimes\mathrm{I})h_Y]\) has norm at most \(\lVert h_Y\rVert\).) Put \[\tag{5} \mathcal B_R:=1+N_R\,\ell_R^2 .\]

Lemma 2.1 (Reductions). It suffices to prove Theorem 1.1 for \(\Lambda\) an interval and \(A\) an interval with \(\partial_\Lambda A\ne\varnothing\), with a constant \(C_{\mathrm{int}}\) bounding \(S_\Omega(A)\); then Theorem 1 holds with \(C_{\mathrm{AL}}=C_{\mathrm{int}}\).

Proof. Let \(\Lambda_1,\dots,\Lambda_k\) be the components of \(\Lambda\) (maximal intervals). Every support of finite graph diameter lies in one component, so \(H=\sum_iH_i\) with \(H_i\) acting on \(\mathcal H_{\Lambda_i}\). The ground eigenspace of \(H\) is the tensor product of the ground eigenspaces of the \(H_i\); if the former is one-dimensional so is each latter, hence \(\Omega=\bigotimes_i\Omega_i\) and \(S_\Omega(A)=\sum_iS_{\Omega_i}(A\cap\Lambda_i)\), while \(\partial_\Lambda A=\bigsqcup_i\partial_{\Lambda_i}(A\cap\Lambda_i)\). Each \(H_i\) inherits (2) with the same \(\Delta\) (restrict the inequality to vectors of the form \(\xi\otimes\bigotimes_{j\ne i}\Omega_j\)). So assume \(\Lambda\) is an interval. Write \(A=\bigsqcup_{a=1}^mA_a\) as a union of maximal subintervals. If some \(A_a=\Lambda\) then \(A=\Lambda\) and \(S(A)=0\). Otherwise each \(A_a\) has at least one boundary edge, and distinct \(A_a\) have distinct boundary edges (two maximal subintervals are separated by at least one site, so their outer cut edges differ). Thus \(m\le\lvert \partial_\Lambda A\rvert\) and subadditivity gives \(S(A)\le\sum_aS(A_a)\le C_{\mathrm{int}}\lvert \partial_\Lambda A\rvert\). ◻

From now on \(\Lambda=\{1,\dots,N\}\) and \(X\subseteq\Lambda\) is an interval. For an integer \(j\ge0\) write \(X^{+j}=\{x\in\Lambda:\operatorname{dist}(x,X)\le j\}\), again an interval.

The marginal tail bound

In words. Fix an interval \(X\) and write \(\Omega=\sum_j\sqrt{p_j}\,u_j\otimes w_j\) for its Schmidt decomposition across \(X\) and the rest. The entanglement spectrum is the probability law of the surprisal \(K=-\log p_j\) drawn with probability \(p_j\); its mean is \(S(X)\). The lemma says that, because of the global gap, \(K\) has exponential tails around its mean with a width that depends only on \(q,R,J/\Delta\): the spectrum of an interval is concentrated, whatever its entropy is.

The mechanism is a tilt. Multiply \(\Omega\) by \(\rho_X^{-u/2}\) (acting on \(X\)) and renormalize: this reweights the Schmidt probabilities to \(p_j^{1-u}/M(u)\), with \(M(u)=\sum_jp_j^{1-u}\) the moment generating function of \(K\). The tilted vector \(\Theta\) is an exact eigenvector of the conjugated Hamiltonian \(FHF^{-1}\). Interactions inside \(X\) or inside its complement do not see the conjugation in expectation (they commute with the marginal, or with \(F\)). Only the boundedly many interactions crossing a cut of \(X\) do, and for each of them the conjugated expectation \(f(z)=\langle\Theta,\sigma^{z}h\sigma^{-z}\Theta\rangle\) is an entire function of the tilt parameter which is bounded on a strip and satisfies \(f(-\bar z)=\overline{f(z)}\). That symmetry kills the linear term of the real part, so the excitation energy of \(\Theta\) is quadratic in \(u\). The gap converts a quadratic energy into a quadratic loss of overlap with \(\Omega\); that overlap is the ratio \(M(u/2)^2/M(u)\). Iterating the resulting inequality \(\log M(u)\le 2\log M(u/2)+Cu^2\) down to \(u\to0\), where \(\log M\) is linear with slope \(S(X)\), gives \(\log M(u)\le uS(X)+Cu^2\): a sub-Gaussian moment bound. Exponential Markov turns it into the tail bound.

In two dimensions the width of the tail grows like the square root of the boundary. In one dimension it is a constant. This constant is what makes both halves of this note work.

Precisely. Let \(X\subseteq\Lambda\) be an interval, \(\rho=\rho_X\) with positive eigenvalues \(p_j\), let \(K\) be the random variable taking the value \(-\log p_j\) with probability \(p_j\), so that \(\mathbb EK=S(X)\), and put \[\tag{6} \vartheta=\frac J\Delta,\qquad u_0=\frac{1}{32\sqrt{(1+\vartheta)\mathcal B_R}}.\]

Lemma 3.1 (Marginal tail bound). For every interval \(X\subseteq\Lambda\) and every real \(u\) with \(\lvert u\rvert\le u_0\), \[\tag{7} \log\operatorname{Tr}\rho_X^{\,1-u}=\log\mathbb E\,\mathrm e^{uK}\le u\,S(X)+512\,\mathrm e\,\vartheta\,\mathcal B_R\,u^2 ,\] where \(\rho_X^{1-u}\) is zero on the kernel of \(\rho_X\). Consequently, for every \(w\ge0\), \[\tag{8} \Pr\{\lvert K-S(X)\rvert>w\}\le 2\mathrm e^{\mathrm e/2}\exp(-u_0w).\]

Proof. Put \(M(u)=\sum_jp_j^{1-u}\), \(L(u)=\log M(u)\); note \(L(0)=0\) and \(L'(0)=S(X)\). Let \(P\) be the support projection of \(\rho\), let \(F=\rho^{-u/2}\) on \(P\mathcal H_X\) and \(F=\mathrm{I}\) on the kernel, and define \[\Theta=\frac{F\Omega}{\sqrt{M(u)}},\qquad \sigma=\operatorname{Tr}_{\Lambda\setminus X}|\Theta\rangle\langle \Theta|=\frac{\rho^{1-u}}{M(u)} .\] \(F\) is invertible on \(\mathcal H_\Lambda\), so \(FHF^{-1}\Theta=E_0\Theta\) and \[\tag{9} \langle\Theta,(H-E_0)\Theta\rangle =\sum_Y\bigl(\langle\Theta,h_Y\Theta\rangle-\langle\Theta,Fh_YF^{-1}\Theta\rangle\bigr).\] A term supported in \(\Lambda\setminus X\) commutes with \(F\) and contributes zero. A term supported in \(X\) contributes zero because \(\operatorname{Tr}\sigma Fh_YF^{-1}=\operatorname{Tr}\sigma h_Y\) by \([F,\sigma]=0\) and cyclicity. So only terms split by \(X\) remain, and there are at most \(N_R\) of them by [eq:count].

Fix such a term \(h=h_Y\). For complex \(z\) let \(\sigma^z=P\exp(z\log(\sigma|_{P\mathcal H_X}))P\) and \(f(z)=\langle\Theta,\sigma^zh\sigma^{-z}\Theta\rangle\), an entire function with \(f(0)=\langle\Theta,h\Theta\rangle\) (because \(\Theta\) lies in the support of \(\sigma\)). At \(z_u=-u/(2(1-u))\) one has, on the support, \(\sigma^{z_u}=M(u)^{-z_u}F\) and \(\sigma^{-z_u}=M(u)^{z_u}F^{-1}\), so \(f(z_u)=\langle\Theta,Fh F^{-1}\Theta\rangle\). On the imaginary axis the powers are unitary on the support and \(\lvert f(iy)\rvert\le J\). For \(\lvert \Re z\rvert\le\tfrac14\) expand \(h\) as in (4) and let \(\Theta=\sum_j\sqrt{s_j}\,e_j\otimes f_j\) be its Schmidt decomposition across \(X\). Then \[f(z)=\sum_\alpha\sum_{j,k}s_j^{1/2+z}s_k^{1/2-z}\, \langle e_j,c_\alpha e_k\rangle\langle f_j,d_\alpha f_k\rangle .\] For \(\lvert \Re z\rvert\le\frac14\), \(\lvert s_j^{1/2+z}s_k^{1/2-z}\rvert\le s_j+s_k\) by the weighted arithmetic–geometric mean inequality, and for any operators \(c,d\) the row and column Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities give \[\tag{10} \sum_{j,k}(s_j+s_k)\,\lvert c_{jk}\rvert\,\lvert d_{jk}\rvert\le 2\lVert c\rVert\,\lVert d\rVert ,\] because \(\sum_k\lvert c_{jk}\rvert\lvert d_{jk}\rvert\le\lVert c\rVert\lVert d\rVert\) for each row \(j\) and likewise for each column. Hence \(\lvert f(z)\rvert\le 2Jq^{2(R+1)}\) on \(\lvert \Re z\rvert\le\frac14\). Hadamard’s three-lines theorem on each of the strips \(0\le\pm\Re z\le\frac14\) gives \(\log\lvert f(x+iy)\rvert\le\log J+4\lvert x\rvert\log(2q^{2(R+1)})\le \log J+8\lvert x\rvert\,\ell_R\), so that \[\tag{11} \lvert f(z)\rvert\le \mathrm eJ\qquad\text{for }\lvert \Re z\rvert\le\delta_R:=\frac1{8\ell_R}.\] Since \(h=h^*\) and \((\sigma^z)^*=\sigma^{\bar z}\), one has \(f(-\bar z)=\overline{f(z)}\). Writing \(f(z)=\sum_nc_nz^n\), this says \(c_n\) is real for even \(n\) and purely imaginary for odd \(n\); so for real \(x\), \(\Re(f(x)-f(0))=\sum_{n\ge2\text{ even}}c_nx^n\), and Cauchy’s estimate \(\lvert c_n\rvert\le\mathrm eJ\delta_R^{-n}\) from (11) gives, for \(\lvert x\rvert\le\delta_R/2\), \[\tag{12} \lvert \Re(f(x)-f(0))\rvert\le 2\mathrm eJ\,x^2\delta_R^{-2}=128\,\mathrm eJ\,\ell_R^2\,x^2 .\] For \(\lvert u\rvert\le u_0\) we have \(\lvert u\rvert\le\frac1{32}\), \(\lvert z_u\rvert\le\lvert u\rvert\) and \(\ell_R\le\sqrt{\mathcal B_R}\), so \(\lvert z_u\rvert\le\delta_R/2\). Since (9) is real, applying [eq:quadratic] to each of the at most \(N_R\) split terms yields \[\tag{13} 0\le\langle\Theta,(H-E_0)\Theta\rangle\le 128\,\mathrm eJ\,N_R\ell_R^2\,u^2 \le 128\,\mathrm eJ\,\mathcal B_R\,u^2 .\] The gap [eq:gap] now gives \(1-\lvert \langle\Omega,\Theta\rangle\rvert^2\le 128\,\mathrm e\,\vartheta\,\mathcal B_Ru^2\le\frac12\), and \(\langle\Omega,\Theta\rangle=M(u/2)/\sqrt{M(u)}\), so \[\frac{M(u/2)^2}{M(u)}\ge 1-128\,\mathrm e\,\vartheta\,\mathcal B_R u^2, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad L(u)\le 2L(u/2)+256\,\mathrm e\,\vartheta\,\mathcal B_Ru^2,\] using \(-\log(1-v)\le2v\) for \(0\le v\le\frac12\). Every halving \(u/2^j\) stays in the admissible interval, so by induction \[L(u)\le 2^jL(u/2^j)+256\,\mathrm e\,\vartheta\,\mathcal B_Ru^2\sum_{i<j}2^{-i} \xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{} uS(X)+512\,\mathrm e\,\vartheta\,\mathcal B_Ru^2 ,\] which is (7). For (8), apply Markov’s inequality to \(\mathrm e^{\pm u_0(K-S)}\): the quadratic term at \(u=\pm u_0\) equals \(512\mathrm e\vartheta\mathcal B_R/(1024(1+\vartheta)\mathcal B_R)\le\mathrm e/2\), so each one-sided tail is at most \(\mathrm e^{\mathrm e/2}\mathrm e^{-u_0w}\). ◻

Remark 3.2 (Rényi entropies near one). For \(0<u\le u_0\) and \(\alpha=1-u\), (7) reads \(S_\alpha(X)=\frac{1}{u}\log\operatorname{Tr}\rho_X^{\alpha}\le S(X)+512\mathrm e\vartheta\mathcal B_R(1-\alpha)\). Since \(S_\alpha\ge S\) for \(\alpha<1\), all Rényi entropies of order \(\alpha\in[1-u_0,1]\) of an interval lie within \(16\mathrm e\sqrt{(1+\vartheta)\mathcal B_R}\) of its von Neumann entropy. This is used in Section 1.

The buffered discount with a constant-width buffer

In words. Here is the one lemma that does the work. Take an interval \(X\) and surround it by \(m\) nested shells: \(X_j=X^{+jD}\) for \(j=1,\dots,m\), each one step of width \(D=\max(R,1)\) thicker than the previous one, and let \(T_j=X_j\setminus X\) be the two flanks. The number \(m\) will be a large constant (of order \(1+J/\Delta\)), so the total buffer \(T=T_m\) has constant width \(mD\) on each side of \(X\). The claim is \[S(X\,|\,T)\ \le\ \tfrac12\,S(X)+C .\] Conditioning \(X\) on a constant number of neighboring sites halves its entropy, up to a constant.

Why should that be? Apply to \(\Omega\) a product \(K=L_m\cdots L_1\) of positive operators, \(L_j\) acting on \(X_j\), each normalized by \(\operatorname{Tr}L_j^{2/a_j}=1\) with \(a_j=2/m\) (so the weights sum to \(2\)), and maximize the norm \(N=\lVert K\Omega\rVert\). Two estimates bound the optimum from opposite sides.

The optimum is small. At the optimum, each filter commutes with the marginal of the filtered vector on its own region (a stationarity computation), which lets one strip the outer filters from any expectation of an operator living inside them. Then the energy of the normalized filtered vector is at most a constant times \(\sum_ja_j^2\times(\text{number of interactions split by }X_j)\): an interaction is split by at most one shell because consecutive shells are \(D\ge R\) apart, and the conjugation of a split term by one filter changes its expectation only quadratically in \(a_j\), by the same kind of row–column Cauchy–Schwarz estimate as in the tail bound. In one dimension the number of split terms is bounded, so the energy is at most \(C/m\). The gap then says the filtered vector overlaps \(\Omega\) to \(1-C/(m\Delta)\). Using that overlap, one peels the filters off one at a time, and each peeled filter \(L_j\) can shrink the norm by at most \(\lVert L_j\Omega\rVert\le\exp(-a_jS(X_j)/2+Ca_j^2)\) by Hölder and the tail bound. So \(-2\log N\ge\sum_ja_jS(X_j)-C\).

The optimum is large. Pin a Schmidt vector \(u_i\) of \(X\), that is, use the trial filters \(L_j=|u_i\rangle\langle u_i|\otimes M_j\) with \(M_j\) a filter on the flank \(T_j\) only. The output is \(\sqrt{p_i}\) times a filtered version of the partner vector \(w_i\), and an interpolation argument shows the flank filters can be chosen so that the norm is at least \(\exp(-\sum_ja_jS_{w_i}(T_j)/2)\). Averaging over \(i\) and using concavity of entropy, \(-2\log N\le S(X)+\sum_ja_jS(T_j)\).

Subtracting, \(\sum_ja_j\,[S(X_j)-S(T_j)]\le S(X)+C\), and \(S(X_j)-S(T_j)\) is the conditional entropy \(S(X|T_j)\), which decreases as the flank grows. With \(\sum_ja_j=2\) this is the discount. Notice what did not happen: no property of the state on the flanks was used beyond their being a constant number of sites, and no gap of any restricted Hamiltonian was invoked.

Precisely. Fix \(D=\max(R,1)\) and an integer \(m\ge4\). For an interval \(X\subseteq\Lambda\) put \[X_0=X,\qquad X_j=X^{+jD}\ (1\le j\le m),\qquad T_j=X_j\setminus X, \qquad a_j=\frac2m .\] Then \(T_1\subseteq\cdots\subseteq T_m\), each \(T_j\) consists of at most two intervals of length at most \(jD\), and \(\sum_ja_j=2\). Define the constants \[\tag{14} C_E:=8\,N_R\,J\,q^{2(R+1)},\qquad m:=\Bigl\lceil\max\Bigl\{4,\ \frac{4C_E}{\Delta},\ 128\sqrt{(1+\vartheta)\mathcal B_R},\ 4096\,\mathrm e\,(1+\vartheta)\,\mathcal B_R\Bigr\}\Bigr\rceil .\] Every entry is at most a constant depending on \(q,R\) times \((1+J/\Delta)\), so \[\tag{15} m\le C_1(q,R)\,(1+\vartheta).\]

Lemma 4.1 (Single splitting). A support \(Y\) with \(\operatorname{diam}_{d_\Lambda}Y\le R\) is split by at most one of the intervals \(X_1,\dots,X_m\). If it is split by \(X_j\), it is contained in \(X_i\) for every \(i>j\) and disjoint from \(X_i\) for every \(i<j\). The same holds for the family \(X_0,\dots,X_m\) with the convention that \(X_0\) may also be split.

Proof. Suppose \(Y\) is split by \(X_j\). It contains a point \(y\notin X_j\) and a point \(x\in X_j\), and all its points are within graph distance \(R\le D\) of \(x\) and of \(y\). Points within distance \(D\) of \(X_j\) lie in \(X_{j+1}\), so \(Y\subseteq X_{j+1}\subseteq X_i\) for \(i>j\). Points of \(X_{j-1}\) are at distance more than \(D\) from every point outside \(X_j\) (because \(X_j=X_{j-1}^{+D}\)), so \(Y\cap X_{j-1}=\varnothing\), and likewise for \(i<j\). A set contained in \(X_i\) or disjoint from \(X_i\) is not split by \(X_i\). ◻

Lemma 4.2 (Buffered discount). With \(m\) as in (14), every interval \(X\subseteq\Lambda\) satisfies \[\tag{16} S(X\,|\,T_m)\ \le\ \frac12\,S(X)+\frac12+\frac{C_E}{\Delta}.\]

Proof. If \(X=\varnothing\) both sides vanish. Assume \(X\ne\varnothing\).

The optimization.

Fix \(\epsilon>0\) so small that \((\dim\mathcal H_{X_j})\epsilon^{2/a_j}<1\) for all \(j\), and maximize \[N=\lVert L_m\cdots L_1\Omega\rVert\] over positive operators \(L_j\) on \(\mathcal H_{X_j}\) (extended by the identity) with \(L_j\ge\epsilon\mathrm{I}\) and \(\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal H_{X_j}}L_j^{2/a_j}=1\). The feasible sets are nonempty and compact, every feasible product is invertible, so a maximum \(N>0\) exists. At an optimum put \(K=L_m\cdots L_1\), \(\phi=K\Omega/N\), and \(\rho_j=\operatorname{Tr}_{\Lambda\setminus X_j}|\phi\rangle\langle \phi|\).

Stationarity.

We claim \([L_j,\rho_j]=0\) for all \(j\), by descending induction on \(j\). The tool is the stripping identity: if \([L_k,\rho_k]=0\) and \(T\) is supported in \(X_k\), then \[\tag{17} \langle\phi,L_kTL_k^{-1}\phi\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(L_k^{-1}\rho_kL_kT)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_kT)=\langle\phi,T\phi\rangle .\] Assume the claim for \(k>j\) and let \(B\) be anti-Hermitian on \(\mathcal H_{X_j}\). Replacing \(L_j\) by \(\mathrm e^{tB}L_j\mathrm e^{-tB}\) preserves both constraints, so the derivative of \(\log\lVert K\Omega\rVert\) at \(t=0\) vanishes. That derivative equals \(\Re\langle\phi,K_{>j}(B-L_jBL_j^{-1})K_{>j}^{-1}\phi\rangle\) with \(K_{>j}=L_m\cdots L_{j+1}\). The operator inside is supported in \(X_j\), hence in \(X_m\); strip \(L_m\) by (17), then \(L_{m-1}\), and so on down to \(L_{j+1}\). Since \(\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_jB)\) is purely imaginary, stationarity gives \(\Re\operatorname{Tr}(L_j^{-1}\rho_jL_jB)=0\) for all anti-Hermitian \(B\), so \(L_j^{-1}\rho_jL_j\) is Hermitian, i.e. equals \(L_j\rho_jL_j^{-1}\), so \(\rho_j\) commutes with \(L_j^2\) and hence with \(L_j\ge0\). No commutation between distinct filters is claimed or used.

In a common eigenbasis let \(l_i\), \(p_i\) be the eigenvalues of \(L_j\), \(\rho_j\), and \(x_i=l_i^{2/a_j}\), so \(\sum_ix_i=1\), \(x_i\ge\epsilon^{2/a_j}\). A diagonal variation and the same stripping give \(d\log N=\frac{a_j}2\sum_ip_i\,dx_i/x_i\). The Kuhn–Tucker conditions on the simplex with the floor say that \(p_i/x_i\) is a common value \(\lambda>0\) on coordinates above the floor and at most \(\lambda\) on floored coordinates (there is a free coordinate by the dimensional slack, and \(\lambda>0\) because moving mass from a zero-probability free coordinate to a positive-probability floored one would increase \(N\)). Hence \(x_i=\max\{\epsilon^{2/a_j},p_i/\lambda\}\) and, since taking a maximum with a common floor contracts differences of logarithms, \[\tag{18} \Bigl|\log\frac{l_i}{l_k}\Bigr|\le\frac{a_j}{2}\Bigl|\log\frac{p_i}{p_k}\Bigr| \qquad(p_i,p_k>0).\]

Energy of the optimized vector.

Since \(KHK^{-1}\phi=E_0\phi\), \[\langle\phi,(H-E_0)\phi\rangle=\sum_Y\Re\bigl(\langle\phi,h_Y\phi\rangle-\langle\phi,Kh_YK^{-1}\phi\rangle\bigr).\] If \(Y\) is split by no \(X_j\) (\(1\le j\le m\)), then by Lemma 6 each \(X_j\) either contains \(Y\) or is disjoint from it; strip the containing filters by (17) from the outside in, and commute the disjoint ones through \(h_Y\): the term vanishes. If \(Y\) is split by \(X_j\), the same procedure leaves exactly \(\langle\phi,h_Y\phi\rangle-\langle\phi,L_jh_YL_j^{-1}\phi\rangle\). Let \(\phi=\sum_i\sqrt{p_i}\,e_i\otimes f_i\) be the Schmidt decomposition across \(X_j\) with \(e_i\) in the common eigenbasis, and \(h_{ik}=\langle e_i\otimes f_i,h_Y(e_k\otimes f_k)\rangle\). Pairing \((i,k)\) with \((k,i)\) and using \(h_{ki}=\overline{h_{ik}}\), \[\Re\langle\phi,L_jh_YL_j^{-1}\phi\rangle-\langle\phi,h_Y\phi\rangle =\sum_{i,k:\,p_ip_k>0}\sqrt{p_ip_k}\,\bigl(\cosh(\log(l_i/l_k))-1\bigr)\Re h_{ik}.\] Put \(z=\frac12\log(p_i/p_k)\). By [eq:clip] and \(a_j\le\frac12\), \(\cosh(\log(l_i/l_k))-1\le\cosh(a_jz)-1\le\frac12a_j^2z^2\cosh(a_jz)\le\frac12a_j^2z^2\cosh(z/2)\), while \(\sqrt{p_ip_k}=(p_i+p_k)/(2\cosh z)\). Since \(\sup_z z^2\cosh(z/2)/(4\cosh z)\le1\), the coefficient is at most \(a_j^2(p_i+p_k)\). Expanding \(h_Y\) by (4) and using [eq:rowcolumn] for each product term gives \[\bigl|\Re\langle\phi,L_jh_YL_j^{-1}\phi\rangle-\langle\phi,h_Y\phi\rangle\bigr| \le 2a_j^2\,q^{2(R+1)}J .\] Summing over the at most \(N_R\) terms split by each \(X_j\), \[\tag{19} 0\le\langle\phi,(H-E_0)\phi\rangle\le\sum_{j=1}^m N_R\cdot 2a_j^2q^{2(R+1)}J =\frac{8N_RJq^{2(R+1)}}{m}=\frac{C_E}{m}.\] By (2) and [eq:CE], \[\tag{20} 1-\lvert \langle\Omega,\phi\rangle\rvert^2\le\frac{C_E}{m\Delta}\le\frac14 .\] The same bound holds for the optimizer of every prefix \(L_j\cdots L_1\), \(1\le j\le m\), since its energy is bounded by the same sum restricted to \(i\le j\).

Upper bound on the optimum.

Let \(N_j\) be the optimum for the prefix \(1,\dots,j\) at the same floor, \(N_0=1\), and let \(L_j\cdots L_1\) be an optimizer for prefix \(j\) with \(K_{<j}=L_{j-1}\cdots L_1\). By [eq:overlap] for that prefix and Cauchy–Schwarz, \[\sqrt{1-C_E/(m\Delta)}\;N_j\le\lvert \langle\Omega,L_jK_{<j}\Omega\rangle\rvert =\lvert \langle L_j\Omega,K_{<j}\Omega\rangle\rvert\le\lVert L_j\Omega\rVert\,N_{j-1}.\] For any feasible \(L_j\), Schatten–Hölder with exponents \(\frac1{1-a_j}\) and \(\frac1{a_j}\) and the constraint \(\operatorname{Tr}L_j^{2/a_j}=1\) give \[\lVert L_j\Omega\rVert^2=\operatorname{Tr}\rho_{X_j}L_j^2\le\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}\rho_{X_j}^{1/(1-a_j)}\bigr)^{1-a_j}.\] Apply Lemma 3.1 to the interval \(X_j\) with \(u=-a_j/(1-a_j)\), which is admissible because \(\lvert u\rvert\le 2a_j=4/m\le u_0\) by (14). Using \((1-a_j)u^2\le 2a_j^2\), \[\log\lVert L_j\Omega\rVert^2\le -a_jS(X_j)+1024\,\mathrm e\,\vartheta\,\mathcal B_R\,a_j^2 .\] Multiplying the recursion over \(j\) and using \(-\frac12\log(1-v)\le v\) for \(v\le\frac12\), \[\log N\le-\frac12\sum_ja_jS(X_j)+512\,\mathrm e\,\vartheta\,\mathcal B_R\sum_ja_j^2+\frac{mC_E}{m\Delta} \le -\frac12\sum_ja_jS(X_j)+\frac12+\frac{C_E}{\Delta},\] because \(\sum_ja_j^2=4/m\le 1/(1024\mathrm e\vartheta\mathcal B_R)\) by (14). That is, \[\tag{21} -2\log N\ \ge\ \sum_{j=1}^ma_jS(X_j)-1-\frac{2C_E}{\Delta}.\] The constants in (21) do not depend on the floor \(\epsilon\).

Lower bound on the optimum by Schmidt pinning.

Let \(\epsilon\downarrow0\). The optima converge to the optimum \(N\) over nonnegative filters with the same trace constraints (the feasible sets are compact and every nonnegative feasible operator is a limit of strictly positive feasible ones), and (21) persists. Write \(\Omega=\sum_i\sqrt{p_i}\,u_i\otimes w_i\) with \(p_i>0\) for the Schmidt decomposition across \(X\). For fixed \(i\) use the trial filters \[L_j=|u_i\rangle\langle u_i|\otimes M_j,\qquad M_j\ge0\text{ on }\mathcal H_{T_j},\qquad\operatorname{Tr}M_j^{2/a_j}=1,\] which are feasible (as \(\operatorname{Tr}_{X_j}L_j^{2/a_j}=\operatorname{Tr}M_j^{2/a_j}=1\)) and give \(N\ge\sqrt{p_i}\,N_i\), where \(N_i=\sup\lVert M_m\cdots M_1w_i\rVert\) over such \(M_j\). We claim \[\tag{22} \log N_i\ge-\frac12\sum_ja_jS_{w_i}(T_j).\] Fix invertible feasible \(M_j\) and consider \(F_i(z)=M_m^z\cdots M_1^zw_i\) on \(0\le\Re z\le1\), bounded and analytic. On \(\Re z=0\) the factors are unitary and \(\lVert F_i(iy)\rVert=1\). On \(\Re z=1\) put \(U_j=M_j^{iy}\), \(V_j=U_j\cdots U_1\), \(V_0=\mathrm{I}\); moving the unitaries left, \[M_m^{1+iy}\cdots M_1^{1+iy}=(U_m\cdots U_1)\,(V_{m-1}^*M_mV_{m-1})\cdots(V_0^*M_1V_0).\] Each conjugated factor is positive, supported in \(T_j\) (the conjugating unitaries live on the smaller nested flanks), and has the same trace-power normalization, so \(\lVert F_i(1+iy)\rVert\le N_i\). Testing against unit vectors and applying the scalar three-lines theorem gives \(\log\lVert F_i(x)\rVert\le x\log N_i\) for \(0\le x\le1\); differentiating at \(x=0^+\) yields \(\sum_j\langle w_i,\log M_j\,w_i\rangle\le\log N_i\). Taking \(M_j=\bigl((1-\delta)\rho_{w_i,T_j}+\delta\mathrm{I}/\dim\mathcal H_{T_j}\bigr)^{a_j/2}\) and letting \(\delta\downarrow0\), the left side tends to \(-\frac12\sum_ja_jS_{w_i}(T_j)\), proving (22). Hence \(-2\log N\le-\log p_i+\sum_ja_jS_{w_i}(T_j)\) for every \(i\); average with weights \(p_i\) and use concavity of the entropy (the \(p_i\)-average of \(\rho_{w_i,T_j}\) is \(\rho_{T_j}\)): \[\tag{23} -2\log N\le S(X)+\sum_{j=1}^ma_jS(T_j).\]

Conclusion.

Subtract (23) from (21): \(\sum_ja_j\bigl(S(X_j)-S(T_j)\bigr)\le S(X)+1+2C_E/\Delta\). Here \(S(X_j)-S(T_j)=S(XT_j)-S(T_j)=S(X|T_j)\), and \(S(X|T_j)\ge S(X|T_m)\) for \(j\le m\) by strong subadditivity (conditioning on the larger flank \(T_m\supseteq T_j\) decreases conditional entropy). With \(\sum_ja_j=2\) this gives \(2S(X|T_m)\le S(X)+1+2C_E/\Delta\). ◻

Closing the argument

In words. The discount says that \(S(X|T)\le\frac12S(X)+C\) where \(T\) is a buffer of a constant number of sites. But a conditional entropy cannot be very negative: by the Araki–Lieb triangle inequality \(S(XT)\ge S(X)-S(T)\), so \(S(X|T)\ge S(X)-2S(T)\). Putting the two together, \(S(X)-2S(T)\le\frac12S(X)+C\), hence \(S(X)\le 4S(T)+2C\), and \(S(T)\) is at most the number of sites of \(T\) times \(\log q\). That is the area law.

Nothing else is needed. In two dimensions the same two inequalities give \(S(X)\le 4S(T)+Cr\) for a box \(X\) of side \(r\) with a buffer \(T\) of width proportional to \(r\); the buffer is an annulus of area \(\sim r^2\) whose entropy is as unknown as that of \(X\), and the inequality is useless. That is the precise point at which the two-dimensional proof must bring in everything else.

Precisely.

Proof of Theorem 1.1. By Lemma 3 it suffices to bound \(S(X)\) for an interval \(X\) in an interval domain. Let \(T=T_m\) be the buffer of Lemma 7; it has at most \(2mD\) sites. The Araki–Lieb inequality \(S(XT)\ge S(X)-S(T)\) gives \(S(X|T)\ge S(X)-2S(T)\), and [eq:discount] gives \(S(X|T)\le\frac12S(X)+\frac12+C_E/\Delta\). Hence \[\tag{24} S(X)\le 4S(T)+1+\frac{2C_E}{\Delta}\le 8mD\log q+1+\frac{2C_E}{\Delta}=:C_{\mathrm{int}}.\] By [eq:mbound] and (14), \(C_{\mathrm{int}}\le C_0(q,R)(1+J/\Delta)\) with, for instance, \(C_0(q,R)=8C_1(q,R)\max(R,1)\log q+1+16N_Rq^{2(R+1)}\). ◻

Remark 8 (What the constant depends on). The gap enters only through \(\vartheta=J/\Delta\), linearly. The dependence on the local dimension and range is through \(N_R\,q^{2(R+1)}\), which for nearest-neighbor interactions (\(R=1\)) is of order \(q^4\); no attempt has been made to optimize it. By comparison, the bound of Arad–Kitaev–Landau–Vazirani is \(\tilde O(\log^3(q)/\Delta)\) for \(R=1\), so the present argument matches the linear dependence on the inverse gap and is worse in \(q\).

Remark 9 (What was used). The proof used the global gap in exactly two places: in Lemma 4 applied at the cuts of the shells \(X_j\), and to convert the energy bound (19) into the overlap bound (20). No Lieb–Robinson bound, no spectral filter, no approximate ground-state projector, no Chebyshev polynomial, no bootstrapping of a Schmidt-rank bound appears. The entropy itself enters only through operator norms of filtered vectors.

The long route, for comparison

In words. It is instructive to see what the two-dimensional proof would do in one dimension if one refused to use the Araki–Lieb shortcut, because this is where each piece of the big machine would be used, and because the amplification and two-family steps reappear, in spirit, in Part .

Sub-volume exponent. Split an interval of length \(Mr\) into \(M\) children of length \(r\) and add them up in a random order. A child whose constant-width buffer has already been added gets the discount, which happens with a fixed probability \(p_0\). This gives \(F(Mr)\le(1-p_0/4)M\,F(r)+C M\) for the maximal interval entropy \(F\), hence \(F(r)\le Cr^{e_0}\) with \(e_0<1\). In two dimensions the analogous recursion gives \(r^{1+e_0}\), which is above the boundary \(r\); in one dimension \(r^{e_0}\) is already below the diameter \(r\), which is all the next step needs.

Amplification. The amplification proposition of the source converts an information bound \(I(X:\text{far})\le n^{1-\varepsilon}\) into \(I(X:J)\le n^{-M}\) for all \(J\) beyond a graph distance \(2r\) with \(r=O((n^{1-\varepsilon}+\log n)^{1/\alpha})\), \(\alpha<1\) as close to one as desired, using positive quasi-local constraints built from Kitaev’s spectral filter, a random product of the corresponding contractions, and a polar decomposition. In one dimension the trivial bound \(I(X:\text{anything})\le2S(X)\le Cn^{e_0}\) for an interval \(X\) of length \(n\) already is such an input, with no collar at all; one gets \(I(X:J)\le n^{-M}\) beyond distance \(Cn^{e_0/\alpha}=o(n)\) once \(\alpha>e_0\).

Two families. Cut the half-line to the right of the cut into dyadic intervals \(X_i=[2^i,2^{i+1})\) and alternate their family labels. Each \(X_i\) is separated from all earlier same-family intervals and from the other side of the cut by the gap \(X_{i-1}\), of length \(2^{i-1}\gg 2^{ie_0/\alpha}\). The two-family cancellation lemma then gives \(S(A)\le|D|\log q+\frac12\sum_i2^{-iM}\) with \(|D|=O(1)\).

So the long route also works, with the entire replica scanner replaced by the trivial inequality \(I\le 2S\). We do not pursue it; the direct proof is shorter and gives better constants.

Precisely. We only record the recursion, since the other two steps are literally the source propositions with the stated inputs. Let \(F(r)\) be the supremum of \(S(X)\) over intervals \(X\subseteq\Lambda\) of length at most \(r\) (over all admissible instances), and let \(w_0=mD\) be the buffer width of Lemma 4.2 with discount constant \(C_{\mathrm{buf}}=\frac12+C_E/\Delta\). Let \(M\ge 8(2w_0/r+2)\) and let \(X\) be an interval of length at most \(Mr\). Cut \(X\) into at most \(M\) consecutive children of length at most \(r\) and order them by a uniformly random permutation. Call a child interior if its buffer of width \(w_0\) on each side lies in \(X\); at most \(B:=2\lceil w_0/r\rceil+2\) children are not interior. For an interior child, the event that all children meeting its buffer precede it has probability at least \(p_0=(2\lceil w_0/r\rceil+3)^{-1}\); on that event the chain-rule term is at most \(S(X_i|T_i)\le\frac12S(X_i)+C_{\mathrm{buf}}\), and in every case it is at most \(S(X_i)\). Taking the expectation of the chain rule, \[F(Mr)\le\Bigl[(1-\tfrac{p_0}2)M+\tfrac{p_0}2B\Bigr]F(r)+p_0MC_{\mathrm{buf}} \le\bigl(1-\tfrac{p_0}4\bigr)M\,F(r)+C M ,\] using \(B\le M/2\). With \(\lambda=(1-p_0/4)M\in(1,M)\) for \(r\ge w_0\), iterating at \(r=w_0,Mw_0,M^2w_0,\dots\) gives \(F(M^jw_0)\le C\lambda^j\) and hence \(F(r)\le Cr^{e_0}\) with \(e_0=\log_M\lambda<1\).

The one-dimensional matrix product state approximation

Statement, and the two-line route

In words. Theorem 2 asks for one global vector, not for a bound on each cut. In two dimensions this is a serious gap between hypothesis and conclusion, and the source closes it with a distributed protocol. In one dimension there is a classical bridge: if the Schmidt spectrum at every cut has a fast-decaying tail, then truncating the cuts one after another produces a matrix product state whose error is the sum of the tail masses (Verstraete–Cirac). The marginal tail bound of Section 2 gives exactly such a tail, with an exponential rate \(u_0\) that depends only on \(q,R,J/\Delta\), and the area law fixes the location of the spectrum. So Theorem 2 follows in a few lines, with bond dimension \(CL^{4/u_0}\).

We record this first because it is true and short. The rest of Part  then presents the one-dimensional version of the protocol proof, which is the construction that generalizes to two dimensions and which uses none of this sequential truncation.

Precisely.

Proposition 7.1 (Sequential truncation). Let \(\Omega\) be a unit vector on \(\mathcal H_\Lambda\), \(\Lambda=\{1,\dots,L\}\). For \(1\le k\le L-1\) let \(\Pi_k\) be the projection of \(\mathcal H_{[1,k]}\) onto the span of the \(D\) eigenvectors of \(\rho_{[1,k]}\) with largest eigenvalues, let \(\epsilon_k=\langle\Omega,(\mathrm{I}-\Pi_k\otimes\mathrm{I})\Omega\rangle\) be the discarded mass, and put \[\Phi=(\Pi_1\otimes\mathrm{I})(\Pi_2\otimes\mathrm{I})\cdots(\Pi_{L-1}\otimes\mathrm{I})\,\Omega.\] Then \(\Phi\) is a matrix product state with open boundary conditions and bond dimension at most \(qD\), and \(\lVert \Phi-\Omega\rVert\le\sum_k\sqrt{\epsilon_k}\).

Proof. Write \(P_k=\Pi_k\otimes\mathrm{I}\). Telescoping, \(\Phi-\Omega=\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}P_1\cdots P_{k-1}(P_k-\mathrm{I})\Omega\) and each prefix is a contraction, which gives the error bound. For the bond dimension fix a cut \(k\). The vector \(\Xi=P_{k+1}\cdots P_{L-1}\Omega\) is in the range of \(P_{k+1}\), so \(\Xi=\sum_{b=1}^D|b\rangle_{[1,k+1]}\otimes\xi_b\); expanding \(|b\rangle=\sum_{s=1}^q\beta_{b,s}\otimes|s\rangle_{k+1}\) shows that \(\Xi\) has Schmidt rank at most \(qD\) across the cut \(k\). The remaining factors \(P_1,\dots,P_k\) act on \([1,k]\) and do not increase that rank. A vector with Schmidt rank at most \(qD\) across every cut is a matrix product state of bond dimension at most \(qD\). ◻

Theorem 7.2 (Theorem 2, two-line route). Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2, for every \(L\ge2\) there is a matrix product state \(\Phi\) with bond dimension at most \(q\,\mathrm e^{C_{\mathrm{int}}}\,(32\mathrm e^{\mathrm e/2}L^4)^{1/u_0}\) and \(\min_\theta\lVert \Phi/\lVert \Phi\rVert-\mathrm e^{i\theta}\Omega\rVert\le L^{-1}\), where \(u_0\) is (6) and \(C_{\mathrm{int}}\) is [eq:final].

Proof. Fix a cut \(k\) and \(X=[1,k]\). By Lemma 4, the eigenvalues \(p\) of \(\rho_X\) with \(-\log p>S(X)+w\) have total mass at most \(2\mathrm e^{\mathrm e/2}\mathrm e^{-u_0w}\); the eigenvalues with \(-\log p\le S(X)+w\) number at most \(\mathrm e^{S(X)+w}\). Choose \(w\) with \(2\mathrm e^{\mathrm e/2}\mathrm e^{-u_0w}=(16L^4)^{-1}\) and \(D=\lceil\mathrm e^{C_{\mathrm{int}}+w}\rceil\ge\mathrm e^{S(X)+w}\) (Theorem 1). Then \(\epsilon_k\le(16L^4)^{-1}\) for every \(k\), Proposition 10 gives \(\lVert \Phi-\Omega\rVert\le(L-1)/(4L^2)\le1/(4L)\), hence \(\Phi\ne0\) and \(\lVert \Phi/\lVert \Phi\rVert-\Omega\rVert\le2\lVert \Phi-\Omega\rVert\le L^{-1}\). The bond dimension is \(qD\) with \(\mathrm e^{w}=(32\mathrm e^{\mathrm e/2}L^4)^{1/u_0}\). ◻

Remark 12. The exponent \(4/u_0\) is of order \(\sqrt{(1+J/\Delta)\mathcal B_R}\). Hastings’ original construction gives a polynomial bond dimension with an exponent that is exponential in \(J/\Delta\); here the exponent is a square root. This is a byproduct of the tail bound, not of the protocol below.

Plan of the protocol route

In words. The protocol proof has four layers, and we keep all four.

  1. Information at logarithmic separation. Two regions of the chain separated by \(D\log L\) sites share at most \(L^{-p}\) nats of mutual information. This is where the gap is used. In two dimensions it took the coarse collar bound, the cell conditional-information lemma and the “angular” induction; here it takes the area law plus one lemma, the information reset (Section 2).

  2. Moves. Three elementary operations change who holds which registers while keeping the global vector close to a reference: a birth (give an interior interval to a new owner), its reverse, a death, and an exchange of assignments between two copies of the ground state inside a window. Each is a contraction built from private maps and one normalized bipartite vector or covector, and each costs \(O(L^{-p/2})\) when the moved region is informationally independent of the other owners (Section 3).

  3. The schedule. A left-to-right sweep with a moving remainder party creates the block owners one by one. The only difficulty is a band owned by the remainder that is wedged between two different owners; it is dissolved by a fixed sequence of nine moves using an auxiliary copy of the ground state, copied from the edge construction of the two-dimensional paper. Every party takes part in a bounded number of moves (Section 10).

  4. Compression and assembly. The distributed construction is a pure linear calculation with unbounded private dimensions. Replacing each bipartite resource by an average of polynomially many random product operators approximates the physical density in trace norm, and the error analysis is dimension-free because each party participates in boundedly many operations. One column of the resulting operator network is a ket network; placing its nodes on the chain gives the matrix product state (Sections 5, 6).

Information at logarithmic separation

Purifications, fidelity, and splitting

In words. Small mutual information \(I(T:E)\) means the joint state of \(T\) and \(E\) is close, in relative entropy, to the product of its marginals. Relative entropy controls fidelity, and fidelity between two mixed states is the largest overlap between their purifications (Uhlmann). So if the third system \(U\) purifies \(TE\), there is an isometry on \(U\) alone that turns the global vector into nearly a product of a purification of \(T\) and a purification of \(E\). Whoever holds \(U\) can do this privately. This is the single mechanism behind births and exchanges.

Precisely. For density operators \(\rho,\sigma\) put \(F(\rho,\sigma)=\lVert \sqrt\rho\sqrt\sigma\rVert_1\) and \(D(\rho\|\sigma)=\operatorname{Tr}\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\), infinite when the support of \(\rho\) is not contained in that of \(\sigma\).

Lemma 8.1 (Fidelity, information, Uhlmann). For normalized finite-dimensional states, \(F(\rho,\sigma)\ge\mathrm e^{-D(\rho\|\sigma)/2}\), and \(D(\rho_{AC}\|\rho_A\otimes\rho_C)=I(A:C)\). If \(\xi\) purifies \(\rho\) on \(\mathcal H\otimes\mathcal K\) and \(\eta\) purifies \(\sigma\) on \(\mathcal H\otimes\mathcal K'\), then after enlarging \(\mathcal K'\) if necessary there is an isometry \(V:\mathcal K\to\mathcal K'\) with \(\lvert \langle\eta,(\mathrm{I}\otimes V)\xi\rangle\rvert=F(\rho,\sigma)\).

Proof. Let \((p_i,|i\rangle)\), \((q_j,|j'\rangle)\) be eigenpairs of \(\rho,\sigma\). If \(D(\rho\|\sigma)<\infty\), \(w_{ij}=p_i\lvert \langle i|j'\rangle\rvert^2\) is a probability on the pairs with \(p_iq_j>0\) and \(D(\rho\|\sigma)=\sum w_{ij}\log(p_i/q_j)\). By convexity of the exponential, \(\operatorname{Tr}\sqrt\rho\sqrt\sigma=\sum_{ij}w_{ij}\exp(-\tfrac12\log(p_i/q_j))\ge\mathrm e^{-D(\rho\|\sigma)/2}\), and the trace norm dominates the trace. The identity for \(I(A:C)\) is the expansion of \(\log(\rho_A\otimes\rho_C)\). The purification statement is Uhlmann’s theorem: the overlaps \(\operatorname{Tr}(\sqrt\rho\sqrt\sigma U)\) over unitaries \(U\) on a common large purifying space have maximum modulus \(\lVert \sqrt\rho\sqrt\sigma\rVert_1\) by polar decomposition, and any purification is obtained from the canonical one by an isometry on the purifying factor. ◻

Lemma 8.2 (Splitting). Let \(T,U,E\) partition \(\Lambda\) and \(b=I_\Omega(T:E)\). There are finite-dimensional spaces \(\mathcal B_T,\mathcal B_E\), an isometry \(V:\mathcal H_U\to\mathcal B_T\otimes\mathcal B_E\) and unit vectors \(s\in\mathcal H_T\otimes\mathcal B_T\), \(s'\in\mathcal H_E\otimes\mathcal B_E\) with \[\lVert (\mathrm{I}_{TE}\otimes V)\Omega-s\otimes s'\rVert\le\sqrt{2(1-\mathrm e^{-b/2})}\le\sqrt b .\]

Proof. \(\rho_{TE}\) is supported in \(\operatorname{supp}\rho_T\otimes\operatorname{supp}\rho_E\) (the projector onto the kernel of a marginal, tensored with the identity, has zero expectation in the positive joint state), so \(D(\rho_{TE}\|\rho_T\otimes\rho_E)=b\) is finite. Purify \(\rho_T\) by \(s\) and \(\rho_E\) by \(s'\), enlarge the purifying factors so that \(\mathcal B_T\otimes\mathcal B_E\) contains an isometric copy of \(\mathcal H_U\), and apply Lemma 13: an isometry \(V\) on \(\mathcal H_U\) attains overlap at least \(\mathrm e^{-b/2}\) with \(s\otimes s'\), with real positive phase. Two unit vectors with overlap \(F\) are at distance \(\sqrt{2(1-F)}\). ◻

The information reset

In words. The area law gives \(I(R:C)=O(1)\) between an interval \(R\) and anything else, never \(o(1)\). The reset lemma turns a constant into an arbitrary inverse power of \(L\) by shrinking the two regions inward by a margin of order \(\log L\). The idea is a two-replica swap. Take two copies of the ground vector, \(\Psi=\Omega\otimes\Omega\), and swap the two copies on \(R\) to get \(\Phi=F_R\Psi\). In \(\Phi\), the core \(R_0\subseteq R\) of one copy and the core \(C_0\subseteq C\) of the same copy come from different original copies, so they are exactly independent. If we can find a unitary \(U\) acting only on the buffer between the cores and sending \(\Psi\) to \(\Phi\) up to \(L^{-p}\), then the joint marginal of the cores in \(\Psi\) equals the product marginal up to \(L^{-p}\), which is the claim.

The unitary is built in three steps. First, \(I(R:C)\le b\) and the splitting lemma give an operator \(W\) on the buffer \(Q\) of both copies with \(\langle\Phi,W\Psi\rangle\ge\mathrm e^{-2b}\). Second, a Gaussian spectral filter applied in both directions, \(M=\int g_h(t)\mathrm e^{it\mathbb H'}W\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H}dt\), projects \(W\Psi\) onto \(\Phi\) and \(W^\dagger\Phi\) onto \(\Psi\) with error \(\mathrm e^{-h\Delta^2/2}\), because \(\Psi,\Phi\) are the unique gapped ground vectors of the two doubled Hamiltonians \(\mathbb H\), \(\mathbb H'=F_R\mathbb HF_R\). Third, a Lieb–Robinson bound localizes \(M\) to within \(\ell=O(1+b+\log L)\) of the buffer and of the edges crossing \(R\), where \(\mathbb H\) and \(\mathbb H'\) differ. The localized operator is almost isometric on \(\Psi\), and its polar unitary does the job. The lemma is proved exactly as in the source; we only insert the one-dimensional locality bound.

Precisely. We need the propagation estimate for a doubled chain. Let \(\mathbb H\) be a Hamiltonian on \(\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}(\mathbb C^q\otimes\mathbb C^q)\) which is a sum of terms \(k_Z\) supported on sets \(Z\) of diameter at most \(R\) with \(\sum_{Z\ni x}\lVert k_Z\rVert\le b_0\) for every \(x\), and \(\tau_t(A)=\mathrm e^{it\mathbb H}A\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H}\).

Lemma 8.3 (Lieb–Robinson bound on a chain). There are \(C,v,\mu>0\) depending only on \(R\) and \(b_0\) such that for every operator \(A\) supported in \(S\subseteq\Lambda\), every site \(y\) and every operator \(B_y\) supported at \(y\), \[\lVert [\tau_t(A),B_y]\rVert\le C\lvert S\rvert\,\lVert A\rVert\,\lVert B_y\rVert\,\mathrm e^{v\lvert t\rvert-\mu\,\operatorname{dist}(S,y)} .\] Consequently, if \(\mathcal P_D\) denotes the conditional expectation onto operators supported in \(D\) (the average over independent Haar unitaries at the sites outside \(D\)), then for every \(\ell\ge0\) \[\lVert \tau_t(A)-\mathcal P_{N_\ell(S)}\tau_t(A)\rVert\le C'\lvert S\rvert\,\lVert A\rVert\,\mathrm e^{v\lvert t\rvert-\mu\ell}.\]

Proof. For \(t\ge0\) put \(c_y(S,t)=\sup\{\lVert [\tau_t(A),B_y]\rVert:\operatorname{supp}A\subseteq S,\lVert A\rVert\le1\}\). Differentiating and using the Jacobi identity, the derivative of \([\tau_t(A),B_y]\) equals \(i[\sum_{Z\cap S\ne\varnothing}\tau_t(k_Z),[\tau_t(A),B_y]]\) plus a sum over \(Z\cap S\ne\varnothing\) of \(-i[\tau_t(A),[\tau_t(k_Z),B_y]]\); the first part is removed by a unitary change of frame, so \(c_y(S,t)\le c_y(S,0)+2\sum_{Z\cap S\ne\varnothing}\lVert k_Z\rVert\int_0^tc_y(Z,u)\,du\). Iterating, and using \(c_y(Z,u)\le2\lVert B_y\rVert\) to see that the remainder after \(m\) iterations tends to zero, gives \[c_y(S,t)\le 2\lVert B_y\rVert\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(2t)^m}{m!} \sum_{\substack{Z_1\cap S\ne\varnothing,\ Z_{i+1}\cap Z_i\ne\varnothing\\ y\in Z_m}} \prod_{i=1}^m\lVert k_{Z_i}\rVert,\] with the \(m=0\) term equal to \(\mathbf 1_{\{y\in S\}}\). Summing the norms of the terms meeting a set of \(n\) sites gives at most \(nb_0\), so the inner sum is at most \(\lvert S\rvert\,b_0\,((R+1)b_0)^{m-1}\le\lvert S\rvert\kappa^m\) with \(\kappa=(R+1)b_0\); and a chain reaching \(y\) needs \(mR\ge\operatorname{dist}(S,y)\). Hence for every \(\mu>0\), \(c_y(S,t)\le 2\lvert S\rvert\lVert B_y\rVert\mathrm e^{-\mu\operatorname{dist}(S,y)}\sum_m(2\kappa\mathrm e^{\mu R}t)^m/m!\), which is the first bound with \(v=2\kappa\mathrm e^{\mu R}\). Negative times are identical. For the second bound, \(\mathcal P_D\) is a product of commuting single-site twirls \(T_y\), telescoping gives \(\lVert B-\mathcal P_DB\rVert\le\sum_{y\notin D}\sup_{U_y}\lVert [B,U_y]\rVert\), and there are at most two sites at each distance \(d>\ell\) from \(S\), so the sum is at most \(2C\lvert S\rvert\lVert A\rVert\mathrm e^{vt}\sum_{d>\ell}\mathrm e^{-\mu d}\). ◻

Lemma 8.4 (Information reset). For every \(p>0\) there is \(C_p<\infty\), depending only on \(p,q,R,J,\Delta\), with the following property for all sufficiently large \(L\). Let \(R,C\subseteq\Lambda\) be disjoint, \(R_0\subseteq R\), \(C_0\subseteq C\), and suppose \(I_\Omega(R:C)\le b\). If \[\operatorname{dist}(R_0,\Lambda\setminus R)>w,\qquad \operatorname{dist}(C_0,\Lambda\setminus C)>w, \qquad w=C_p(1+b+\log L),\] then \(I_\Omega(R_0:C_0)\le L^{-p}\).

Proof. If a core is empty there is nothing to prove. Let \(\Psi=\Omega\otimes\Omega\) on two replicas, \(F_R\) the unitary exchanging the two replicas at the sites of \(R\), \(\Phi=F_R\Psi\), and \(Q=\Lambda\setminus(R\cup C)\).

Overlap. Apply Lemma 14 to \((T,U,E)=(R,Q,C)\): an isometry \(V:\mathcal H_Q\to\mathcal B_R\otimes\mathcal B_C\) and unit vectors \(a\in\mathcal H_R\otimes\mathcal B_R\), \(a'\in\mathcal H_C\otimes\mathcal B_C\) with \(\lvert \langle a\otimes a',\widetilde\Omega\rangle\rvert\ge\mathrm e^{-b/2}\), \(\widetilde\Omega=(\mathrm{I}\otimes V)\Omega\). The largest Schmidt coefficient of \(\widetilde\Omega\) across \(R\mathcal B_R\,|\,C\mathcal B_C\) is therefore at least \(\mathrm e^{-b}\), so \(\operatorname{Tr}\rho_{\widetilde\Omega,R\mathcal B_R}^2\ge\mathrm e^{-2b}\). Define on the two replicas of \(Q\) the Hermitian contraction \(W=(V^\dagger\otimes V^\dagger)F_{\mathcal B_R}(V\otimes V)\), where \(F_{\mathcal B_R}\) swaps the two copies of \(\mathcal B_R\). Since \(V\) acts outside \(R\), the swap identity \(\langle\xi^{\otimes2},F_{A}\xi^{\otimes2}\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\rho_{\xi,A}^2\) gives \[\tag{25} z:=\langle\Phi,W\Psi\rangle=\langle\widetilde\Omega^{\otimes2},F_RF_{\mathcal B_R}\widetilde\Omega^{\otimes2}\rangle =\operatorname{Tr}\rho_{\widetilde\Omega,R\mathcal B_R}^2\ \ge\ \mathrm e^{-2b}.\]

Two-sided Gaussian filtering. Let \(\mathbb H=H\otimes\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{I}\otimes H\) and \(\mathbb H'=F_R\mathbb HF_R\). Both are range-\(R\) Hamiltonians on the doubled chain with uniformly bounded interaction sums, with unique ground vectors \(\Psi\), \(\Phi\), common ground energy \(\mathcal E=2E_0\), and gap at least \(\Delta\). Their difference \(\mathbb H'-\mathbb H\) is supported on the endpoints \(Z_R\) of the edges crossing \(R\) (interior terms of \(R\) are merely exchanged between the two identical replica copies). Put \(B_*=1+b+\log L\), \(r_*=2p+10\), \(h=AB_*\) with \(A\Delta^2/2\ge r_*+30\), and \[g_h(t)=(2\pi h)^{-1/2}\mathrm e^{-t^2/(2h)},\qquad M=\int_{\mathbb R}g_h(t)\,\mathrm e^{it\mathbb H'}W\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H}\,dt .\] \(M\) is a contraction, and by the spectral theorem \(M\Psi=\mathrm e^{-h(\mathbb H'-\mathcal E)^2/2}W\Psi\), \(M^\dagger\Phi=\mathrm e^{-h(\mathbb H-\mathcal E)^2/2}W^\dagger\Phi\), so by the gaps \[\tag{26} \lVert M\Psi-z\Phi\rVert,\ \lVert M^\dagger\Phi-z\Psi\rVert\le\mathrm e^{-h\Delta^2/2}\le\mathrm e^{-10b}L^{-r_*}.\] Truncating the integral to \(\lvert t\rvert\le T=T_0B_*\) costs at most \(2\mathrm e^{-T_0^2B_*/(2A)}\), which is also at most \(\mathrm e^{-10b}L^{-r_*}\) once \(T_0^2/(2A)\ge r_*+30\).

Localization. Write \(\mathrm e^{it\mathbb H'}W\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H}=(\mathrm e^{it\mathbb H'}W\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H'})\,V(t)\) with \(V(t)=\mathrm e^{it\mathbb H'}\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H}\), whose left generator \(G(t)=\mathrm e^{it\mathbb H'}(\mathbb H'-\mathbb H)\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H'}\) is a sum of at most \(N_R\lvert \partial R\rvert\) terms of norm at most \(2J\), each supported within distance \(R\) of a cut edge of \(R\). Lemma 15 applied to \(W\) (support \(Q\), norm one) and to the terms of \(G(t)\) gives Hermitian localized approximants \(W_\ell(t)=\mathcal P_{N_\ell(Q)}\bigl(\mathrm e^{it\mathbb H'}W\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H'}\bigr)\) and \(G_\ell(t)=\mathcal P_{N_\ell(Z_R)}G(t)\) with \[\lVert \mathrm e^{it\mathbb H'}W\mathrm e^{-it\mathbb H'}-W_\ell(t)\rVert+\lVert G(t)-G_\ell(t)\rVert\le CL\,\mathrm e^{v\lvert t\rvert-\mu\ell},\] where the single factor \(L\) bounds \(\lvert Q\rvert\) and \(\lvert \partial R\rvert\). Let \(V_\ell(t)\) be the unitary evolution generated by \(G_\ell(t)\), supported in \(N_\ell(Z_R)\); Duhamel’s formula gives \(\lVert V(t)-V_\ell(t)\rVert\le CL\lvert t\rvert\mathrm e^{v\lvert t\rvert-\mu\ell}\). Hence \(W_\ell(t)V_\ell(t)\) is a contraction supported in \(S:=N_\ell(Q\cup Z_R)\) which approximates the integrand uniformly for \(\lvert t\rvert\le T\) with error \(CL(1+T)\mathrm e^{vT-\mu\ell}\). Take \(\ell=\lceil K_{\mathrm{loc}}B_*\rceil\) with \(K_{\mathrm{loc}}\) fixed large enough (after \(T_0\)) that this error is at most \(\mathrm e^{-10b}L^{-r_*}\), using \(\log L\le B_*\) and \(\log(1+T)\le C+B_*\). Averaging the localized integrands against the truncated Gaussian gives a contraction \(M_{\mathrm{loc}}\) supported in \(S\) with \[\tag{27} \lVert M_{\mathrm{loc}}\Psi-z\Phi\rVert,\ \lVert M_{\mathrm{loc}}^\dagger\Phi-z\Psi\rVert\le C\mathrm e^{-10b}L^{-r_*}.\]

Polar decomposition. Put \(D=M_{\mathrm{loc}}/z\). Then \(\lVert D\rVert\le\mathrm e^{2b}\), \(\lVert D\Psi-\Phi\rVert,\lVert D^\dagger\Phi-\Psi\rVert\le C\mathrm e^{-8b}L^{-r_*}\), and writing \((D^\dagger D-\mathrm{I})\Psi=D^\dagger(D\Psi-\Phi)+(D^\dagger\Phi-\Psi)\), \(\lVert (D^\dagger D-\mathrm{I})\Psi\rVert\le C\mathrm e^{-6b}L^{-r_*}\). The polar partial isometry of the square operator \(D\) on \(\mathcal H_S\) extends to a unitary \(U\) on \(\mathcal H_S\) (its two support spaces have equal dimension), tensored with the identity elsewhere. Since \((\sqrt x-1)^2\le(x-1)^2\) for \(x\ge0\), \(\lVert (U-D)\Psi\rVert=\lVert (\mathrm{I}-\lvert D\rvert)\Psi\rVert\le\lVert (\mathrm{I}-D^\dagger D)\Psi\rVert\), so \[\tag{28} \lVert U\Psi-\Phi\rVert\le C\mathrm e^{-6b}L^{-r_*}\le CL^{-r_*}.\]

Support and conclusion. Every site of \(Q\) is more than \(w\) from \(R_0\) and from \(C_0\). If \(\{x,y\}\) crosses \(R\) with \(x\in R\), \(y\notin R\), then \(y\) is more than \(w\) from \(R_0\) so \(x\) is more than \(w-1\) from \(R_0\); and \(x\notin C\) is more than \(w\) from \(C_0\), so \(y\) is more than \(w-1\) from \(C_0\). Choosing \(C_p>K_{\mathrm{loc}}+3\) makes \(S=N_\ell(Q\cup Z_R)\) disjoint from both cores in both replicas. Thus \(U\) does not change the joint two-replica marginal on \(R_0C_0\). In \(\Phi=F_R\Psi\), the marginal on \(R_0\) and \(C_0\) of the first replica is \(\rho_{R_0}\otimes\rho_{C_0}\), because \(R_0\) was swapped in from the second replica and \(C_0\) was not. By (28) and contractivity of the partial trace, the original marginal \(\rho_{R_0C_0}\) is within trace distance \(CL^{-r_*}\) of \(\rho_{R_0}\otimes\rho_{C_0}\). The continuity bound \(\lvert S(A|B)_\rho-S(A|B)_\sigma\rvert\le C\sqrt\delta\log(\mathrm e\dim\mathcal H_A)\) for \(\delta=\frac12\lVert \rho-\sigma\rVert_1\) , applied to \(I(R_0:C_0)=S(R_0)-S(R_0|C_0)\) with \(\log\dim\mathcal H_{R_0}\le L\log q\), gives \(I_\Omega(R_0:C_0)\le CL^{1-r_*/2}=CL^{-p-4}\le L^{-p}\) for large \(L\). ◻

The one-dimensional separation lemma

In words. Combine the two previous facts. An interval \(R\) has entropy at most \(C_{\mathrm{int}}\), so its mutual information with anything is at most \(2C_{\mathrm{int}}\). Feed this constant into the reset lemma: any interval \(T\) and any set \(E\) at distance of order \(\log L\) from \(T\) are independent up to \(L^{-p}\). This replaces the entire Section 3 of the two-dimensional paper (coarse collar, cell conditional information, angular induction), and it holds for intervals of every length, from a few sites to the whole chain.

Precisely.

Lemma 8.5 (Information at logarithmic separation). For every \(p>0\) and \(k_0\ge1\) there is \(D_p=D_p(q,R,J,\Delta,k_0)\) such that for all sufficiently large \(L\), every \(T\subseteq\Lambda\) that is a union of at most \(k_0\) intervals, and every \(E\subseteq\Lambda\) with \(\operatorname{dist}(T,E)\ge D_p\log L\), \[I_\Omega(T:E)\le L^{-p}.\]

Proof. Let \(b=4k_0C_{\mathrm{int}}\), \(w=C_p(1+b+\log L)\) from Lemma 16, and \(D_p\log L\ge 3w+3\). Put \(R=T^{+(w+1)}\), a union of at most \(k_0\) intervals, \(C=\Lambda\setminus T^{+(2w+2)}\), \(R_0=T\), \(C_0=E\). Then \(\operatorname{dist}(R_0,\Lambda\setminus R)\ge w+1\), \(\operatorname{dist}(C_0,\Lambda\setminus C)\ge w+1\), and \(I(R:C)\le 2S(R)\le 2k_0C_{\mathrm{int}}\le b\) by Theorem 1 (subadditivity over the at most \(k_0\) intervals of \(R\), and \(I(R:C)=S(R)+S(C)-S(RC)\le 2S(R)\) by the Araki–Lieb inequality). Lemma 16 gives the claim. ◻

Remark 8.6 (A route without Lieb–Robinson bounds). The reset lemma is the only place in Part  where a Lieb–Robinson bound is used. It can be replaced by the amplification argument of , which uses exact positive quasi-local constraints with stretched-exponential tails \(\mathrm e^{-cl^\alpha}\), \(\alpha=p/(p+1)\), random products of the associated contractions, and a polar decomposition, and needs no propagation bound for the unitary dynamics. With the area law as input, that argument gives \(I(T:E)\le L^{-M}\) for \(E\) beyond distance \(C(\log L)^{1/\alpha}\) of an interval \(T\). Running the protocol below with blocks of that size yields a matrix product state with bond dimension \(\exp(O((\log L)^{1/\alpha}))\), quasi-polynomial for every fixed \(p\), and polynomial only in the limit. Exactly polynomial bond dimension through the protocol requires the logarithmic separation of Lemma 17.

Distributed constructions and the three moves

In words. Think of a bookkeeping game. There are parties; each owns a set of registers (tensor factors), which may include whole copies of the ground vector held privately. A gate is a linear contraction applied to the registers of a few parties. A gate is private if it touches one party only; private gates are free. A non-private gate is charged only through two numbers: how many parties it involves, and how many such gates each party ever takes part in. In addition every non-private gate must have a polynomial expansion: it must be a sum of polynomially many terms, each a composition of private maps, preparations of normalized vectors shared by two parties, contractions by normalized covectors shared by two parties, and transfers of polynomial-dimensional registers. The dimensions of the shared vectors are unrestricted.

The point of the game is that its final physical density admits a tensor network with polynomial links on the party graph (Section 5), whatever the private dimensions were. So we are free to prepare the ground vector exactly at one party and to use arbitrarily entangled bipartite resources; the price is only paid in participation counts.

A sheet is a full set of site registers with an assignment of owners; its reference vector is a copy of the ground vector placed on those registers in canonical site coordinates (ownership does not permute sites). The three moves change the ownership of a sheet or of two sheets while keeping the actual vector within \(O(L^{-p/2})\) of the product of the reference vectors.

Precisely. A distributed construction of size parameter \(L\) consists of a finite set of parties, each with an initial finite-dimensional register space in a normalized product initial vector, and a sequence of gates, each a linear contraction between specified input and output register spaces of the participating parties, the other registers being spectators. Registers to be discarded are retained until a final partial trace. An allowed monomial is a composition of private contractions, preparations of normalized vectors shared between two participating parties, contractions by normalized covectors shared between two participating parties, and identity transfers of registers of uniformly polynomial dimension. A non-private gate is admissible if it is a sum of uniformly polynomially many allowed monomials with uniformly polynomially bounded scalar coefficients, each monomial containing a bounded number of shared vectors, shared covectors and transfers; a contraction within \(L^{-a}\) of such a sum, for a fixed \(a>0\), is also admissible after rescaling by \(1+L^{-a}\). A bounded change is a sequence of a bounded number of admissible gates on a bounded number of parties.

A sheet is \(\mathcal H_\Lambda=\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}\mathbb C^q\) with an owner assigned to each site; its reference vector is \(\Omega\) in these registers.

Lemma 9.1 (Birth and death). In a sheet, let \(T\) be a set of sites owned by \(P_\circ\), and let \(E=\{x:\text{the owner of }x\text{ is not }P_\circ\}\), \(U=\Lambda\setminus(T\cup E)\). If \(I_\Omega(T:E)\le L^{-2p}\), then the change of owner of \(T\) from \(P_\circ\) to any party \(Q_\circ\) (a birth) is a bounded change involving \(P_\circ,Q_\circ\) only, consisting of private maps and one shared normalized vector, with reference-vector error at most \(L^{-p}\). The reverse change (a death: \(T\) returns from \(Q_\circ\) to \(P_\circ\)) is a bounded change with the same error whenever the ownership and information conditions hold in the sheet after the death; it uses one shared normalized covector instead.

Proof. Let \(V,s,s'\) be given by Lemma 8.2 for \((T,U,E)\), put \(\mathcal V=\mathrm{I}_{TE}\otimes V\) and \(\Pi=|s\rangle\langle s|_{T\mathcal B_T}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{E\mathcal B_E}\). All of \(T,U\) belong to \(P_\circ\). At \(P_\circ\) apply \(V\) on \(U\); contract \(T\mathcal B_T\) with \(\langle s|\) (private, both at \(P_\circ\)); supply the shared vector \(s\) on \(T\mathcal B_T\) with \(T\) now owned by \(Q_\circ\) and \(\mathcal B_T\) by \(P_\circ\); apply \(V^\dagger\) at \(P_\circ\). The composite is the Hermitian contraction \(B=\mathcal V^\dagger\Pi\mathcal V\) and \(\lVert (B-\mathrm{I})\Omega\rVert\le\lVert (\Pi-\mathrm{I})\mathcal V\Omega\rVert=\lVert (\Pi-\mathrm{I})(\mathcal V\Omega-s\otimes s')\rVert\le L^{-p}\). For the death, apply \(V\) at \(P_\circ\), use the shared covector \(\langle s|\) on \(T\mathcal B_T\) held by \(Q_\circ,P_\circ\), reinsert \(s\) wholly at \(P_\circ\), and apply \(V^\dagger\): the same operator \(B\) with reversed ownership. ◻

Lemma 9.2 (Two-sheet exchange). Consider two sheets and an interval \(Y\subseteq\Lambda\). Inside \(Y\) exchange the two ownership assignments (sheet 1 at \(x\in Y\) gets the owner sheet 2 had, and conversely); outside \(Y\) keep both. Let \(P_\circ\) be a party, \[Z=\{x:\text{the two owners of }x\text{ are not both }P_\circ\},\qquad T=Z\cap Y,\quad E=Z\setminus Y,\quad U=\Lambda\setminus Z .\] If \(I_\Omega(T:E)\le L^{-2p}\), the exchange is implemented by renaming of registers (no register changes party) and private contractions at \(P_\circ\), with error at most \(4L^{-p}\) between the products of the old and of the new reference vectors.

Proof. Let \(\mathcal R\) rename the two sheets inside \(Y\): a private identification of tensor factors, since the new owner of sheet 1 at \(x\in Y\) is the old owner of sheet 2 there. In canonical coordinates \(\mathcal R\) acts as the swap \(F_{Y}\) of the two sheets on \(Y\). All registers of \(U\) on both sheets are held by \(P_\circ\) before and after. Let \(V,s,s'\) be given by Lemma 14 for \((T,U,E)\), \(\mathcal V=\mathrm{I}_{TE}\otimes V\), and let \(D_U=(V^{\otimes2})^\dagger F_{\mathcal B_T}V^{\otimes2}\) on the two copies of \(U\), a private contraction at \(P_\circ\) (apply \(V\) to both copies, swap the two \(\mathcal B_T\) factors, apply the adjoints). With \(A=Y\cap U\), after \(\mathcal R\) apply the private swap \(F_A\) and then \(D_U\). Since \(Y=T\sqcup A\), the net action on the two copies is \[D_UF_AF_Y=D_UF_T=(\mathcal V^{\otimes2})^\dagger F_{T\mathcal B_T}\mathcal V^{\otimes2},\] using that \(F_T\) acts outside \(U\) and commutes through \(V^{\otimes2}\). Put \(\zeta=s\otimes s'\) and \(\delta=\lVert \mathcal V\Omega-\zeta\rVert\le L^{-p}\). Then \(\lVert (\mathcal V\Omega)^{\otimes 2}-\zeta^{\otimes 2}\rVert\le2\delta\), \(\zeta^{\otimes2}\) is fixed by \(F_{T\mathcal B_T}\) (it swaps two identical factors \(s\)), and \((\mathcal V^{\otimes2})^\dagger\mathcal V^{\otimes2}=\mathrm{I}\), so \[\bigl\|(\mathcal V^{\otimes2})^\dagger F_{T\mathcal B_T}\mathcal V^{\otimes2}\Omega^{\otimes2}-\Omega^{\otimes2}\bigr\| \le\lVert (F_{T\mathcal B_T}-\mathrm{I})\bigl((\mathcal V\Omega)^{\otimes 2}-\zeta^{\otimes 2}\bigr)\rVert\le4\delta .\] In canonical coordinates both the old and the new reference vector are \(\Omega\otimes\Omega\) (the sheets are relabeled, not moved), which is the claim. ◻

Remark 21. In the two-dimensional source these lemmas carry encoded holes and tags at the points where three owners meet. On a line, the closure of two intervals with different owners meets at most two owners at any point, so there are no such points and no encodings. This is the first of the two reasons why the adaptive patch constraints of are not needed in one dimension.

The sweep protocol

In words. Here is the schedule. Divide the chain into consecutive blocks of \(t\) sites, where \(t=20w\) and \(w\) is the logarithmic separation of Lemma 8.5; the last block is whatever is left over, of length less than \(3t\). At the start, a single party \(R_0\) holds everything. At step \(k\) the picture is \[P_1\,|\,P_2\,|\cdots|\,P_k\,|\,R_k ,\] with \(P_i\) owning block \(i\) and the remainder party \(R_k\) owning the rest of the chain. Two births, both allowed because they leave a margin of \(w\) to any other owner, create the next remainder \(R_{k+1}\) on everything beyond block \(k+1\) plus a margin, and the next block owner \(P_{k+1}\) on the interior of block \(k+1\). What remains of \(R_k\) are two bands of width \(w\): one wedged between \(P_k\) and \(P_{k+1}\), one between \(P_{k+1}\) and \(R_{k+1}\). Each band must be dissolved, and this is the only nontrivial step.

A band \(A\) wedged between two different owners \(C\) (left) and \(B\) (right) cannot simply die into \(B\): the band touches \(C\), so it is not informationally independent of the non-\(B\) owners. The two-dimensional paper solves this along every edge of every square with a fixed sequence of moves using an auxiliary copy of the ground vector, and the sequence transplants verbatim. In units of the band width, with the band at \(0<x<1\), \(C\) to the left and \(B\) to the right:

  1. Create a fresh copy of the ground vector, all of it owned by \(C\). On it, birth \(B\) on \((-6,-1)\), then \(A\) on \((-5,-2)\), then \(C\) on \((-4,-3)\). The auxiliary word is \(C\,|\,B\,|\,A\,|\,C\,|\,A\,|\,B\,|\,C\).

  2. Exchange the two sheets inside the window \((-7,-\tfrac72)\). Both ends of the window are in places where both sheets are owned by \(C\), and the only non-\(C\) material inside the window is separated from the non-\(C\) material outside it by a margin \(w\). The main sheet now reads \(C\,|\,B\,|\,A\,|\,C\,|\,A\,|\,B\) with interfaces at \(-6,-5,-4,0,1\), and the auxiliary sheet is retired.

  3. On the main sheet: the \(C\) band \((-4,0)\) is flanked by \(A\) on both sides, so it dies into \(A\); the \(A\) band \((-5,1)\) is then flanked by \(B\) on both sides and dies into \(B\). The word is \(C\,|\,B\) with the interface at \(-6\). Finally birth \(C\) on \((-3,0)\) inside \(B\) and let the \(B\) band \((-6,-3)\) die into \(C\): the interface is back at \(0\) and the band belongs to \(B\).

Each move in this list has its changed region at distance at least \(w\) from every other owner, so Lemma 8.5 supplies the information hypothesis of Lemma 9.1 or 20. Only three parties take part. Since a party is born once, dissolves the bands on its two sides once, and is otherwise a spectator, every party participates in a bounded number of moves.

Why a sweep rather than the dyadic hierarchy of the source? Because the final network must be placed on the chain. A link of the party network joins two parties, and every link crossing a given bond of the chain contributes a factor to that bond’s dimension. In a dyadic hierarchy, every scale has a link crossing every bond (the tree of intervals has cutwidth \(\log L\)), so the bond dimension would be polynomial to the power \(\log L\). In the sweep every link joins parties attached to blocks at most two apart, and a bounded number of links cross each bond.

Precisely. Fix \(p=12\). Let \(D_{24}\) be the constant of Lemma 17 with exponent \(2p=24\) and \(k_0=1\), and put \[\tag{29} w=\lceil D_{24}\log L\rceil,\qquad t=20w,\qquad k_*=\max\{k\ge0:\ L-kt\ge 3t\},\] with the blocks \(B_i=[(i-1)t+1,\,it]\) for \(1\le i\le k_*\) and the last block \(B_{k_*+1}=[k_*t+1,L]\), of length in \([3t,4t)\). We assume \(L\) large enough that \(t\ge 20\) and \(k_*\ge0\) (small \(L\) are handled in Section 6). For a band \([a,a+w)\) with \(C\) owning \([a-8w,a)\) and \(B\) owning \([a+w,a+2w)\) (and possibly more), write \(x=(\cdot-a)/w\) for the scaled coordinate.

Construction 10.1 (Band dissolution). Let the main sheet read \(C\,|\,A\,|\,B\) near \(a\): \(C\) on \(x<0\) down to at least \(x=-8\), \(A\) on \(0<x<1\), \(B\) on \(x>1\) up to at least \(x=2\), where \(A,B,C\) are three distinct parties. Perform:

If there is no left owner \(C\) (the band starts at site \(1\)), replace the whole construction by a single death of the band into \(B\).

Lemma 10.2 (The band dissolution is a bounded change). For all sufficiently large \(L\), each of the nine moves in Construction 22 satisfies the information hypothesis of Lemma 19 or Lemma 20 with exponent \(2p=24\), so that the construction is a bounded change on the parties \(A,B,C\) with total reference-vector error at most \(12L^{-12}\). Construction 10.3 (The sweep). Initially one party \(R_0\) holds the sheet. For \(k=0,1,\dots,k_*-1\), with \(a=kt+1\) the first site of block \(k+1\) and the word \(P_1|\cdots|P_k|R_k\):

  1. Birth \(R_{k+1}\) on \([a+t+w,L]\) inside \(R_k\).

  2. Birth \(P_{k+1}\) on \([a+w,a+t-1]\) inside \(R_k\).

  3. Dissolve the band \([a,a+w-1]\) (owner \(R_k\)) between \(C=P_k\) and \(B=P_{k+1}\) by Construction 22; if \(k=0\) let the band die into \(P_1\).

  4. Dissolve the band \([a+t,a+t+w-1]\) (owner \(R_k\)) between \(C=P_{k+1}\) and \(B=R_{k+1}\) by Construction 22.

At the end set \(P_{k_*+1}=R_{k_*}\), which owns exactly the last block.

Proposition 10.4 (Properties of the sweep). For all sufficiently large \(L\), Construction 10.3 is a distributed construction with the following properties.

  1. There are \(2k_*+2\le 2L\) parties. The initial vector is a product by party: \(R_0\) holds \(\Omega\), and every auxiliary copy of \(\Omega\) is initialized privately at the party \(C\) of its band dissolution.

  2. There are at most \(20k_*\le 2L\) non-private gates. Each involves at most three parties and is admissible with one shared vector or covector per monomial. Each party participates in at most \(40\) non-private gates over its lifetime.

  3. The final vector \(\Psi\) satisfies \(\lVert \Psi\rVert\le1\) and \(\lVert \Psi-\Omega\otimes\Xi\rVert\le 26\,L^{-11}\), where \(\Xi\) is the tensor product of the retired auxiliary copies of \(\Omega\) in their final registers and \(\lVert \Xi\rVert=1\). At the end, party \(P_i\) owns exactly the raw registers of block \(B_i\), of dimension \(q^{\lvert B_i\rvert}\le q^{4t}=L^{O(1)}\), and no other party owns a raw register.

Proof. At step \(k\) the word is \(P_1|\cdots|P_k|R_k\) with \(R_k\) owning \([a,L]\), \(a=kt+1\). Move (1) has changed region \([a+t+w,L]\) and \(E=[1,a-1]\) at distance \(t+w\); move (2) has changed region \([a+w,a+t-1]\) and \(E=[1,a-1]\cup[a+t+w,L]\) at distance \(w\). Both are births by Lemma 19 with Lemma 17. After them \(R_k\) owns the two bands \([a,a+w-1]\) and \([a+t,a+t+w-1]\). For (3), \(C=P_k\) owns block \(k\), of length \(t=20w\ge8w\) to the left of \(a\), and \(B=P_{k+1}\) owns \([a+w,a+t-1]\), of length \(19w\ge2w\), so Lemma 23 applies (for \(k=0\), the death of \([1,w]\) into \(P_1\) has \(E=[t+1,L]\) at distance \(t-w\)). For (4), \(C=P_{k+1}\) owns \([a+w,a+t-1]\) of length \(19w\ge8w\) and \(B=R_{k+1}\) owns \([a+t+w,L]\) of length at least \(3t-w\ge2w\). After (4) the word is \(P_1|\cdots|P_{k+1}|R_{k+1}\). The parties are \(R_0,\dots,R_{k_*}\) and \(P_1,\dots,P_{k_*}\), with \(P_{k_*+1}=R_{k_*}\). The number of moves per step is at most \(2+2\cdot9=20\), so at most \(20k_*\) in total, and the last block has length in \([3t,4t)\).

Participation: \(R_k\) takes part in steps \(k-1\) (as the born party in (1) and as \(B\) in (4)) and \(k\) (as \(P_\circ\) in (1), (2) and as \(A\) in (3), (4)); \(P_k\) takes part in step \(k-1\) (born in (2), \(B\) in (3), \(C\) in (4)) and step \(k\) (\(C\) in (3)). Each band dissolution has nine moves and an exchange that is private; so no party exceeds \(2\cdot(2+2\cdot9)=40\) gates. Every birth or death is a single gate on two parties with one shared vector or covector, and every exchange consists of private gates only.

Errors: every move takes the current actual vector within the stated error of the next reference vector, which is the tensor product of \(\Omega\) on the main sheet and \(\Omega\) on each auxiliary sheet in canonical coordinates (Lemmas 19, 20; retired sheets are spectators of norm one). Telescoping a product of contractions bounds the accumulated error by the sum of the per-move errors: two births and two dissolutions per step cost at most \((2+2\cdot12)L^{-12}\), so the total is at most \(26k_*L^{-12}\le 26L^{-11}\). The initial vector has norm one and every gate is a contraction, so \(\lVert \Psi\rVert\le1\). ◻

Remark 26 (Why no patches). In the source, squares of size below \(K_0\log L\) are repainted by “small-patch rewrites” that use finite-rank cylinder projections of polynomial rank. Here the final owners hold blocks of \(O(\log L)\) sites, whose register dimension \(q^{O(\log L)}\) is polynomial, and the compression theorem below accepts polynomial-dimensional physical outputs. Converting a block tensor into single-site tensors is then linear algebra (Section 6). This is the second reason the adaptive patch constraint is not needed.

Compression and column extraction

In words. The sweep is a pure linear calculation ending in a partial trace over everything but the block registers. Its gates involve shared vectors of enormous dimension (purifications of half-chain marginals). The compression theorem says that if we replace, in the density calculation \(\rho=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{private}}|\Psi\rangle\langle \Psi|\), each shared vector \(|\eta\rangle\langle \tilde\eta|\) by an average of \(k\) independent random product operators \(U_j\otimes V_j\) built from its Schmidt data and Gaussian coefficients, then for polynomial \(k\) the physical density changes by at most \(L^{-p}\) in trace norm. Expanding the error by which positions use a centered correction, each correction position involves only the two endpoint parties, and by bounded participation only boundedly many gates have to be opened for each corrected position; a Gaussian second-moment computation then shows that the expected trace norm of a term with \(s\) corrections is \(k^{-s/2}\) times a fixed polynomial per correction. Nothing in this argument depends on the private dimensions or on the Schmidt ranks of the shared vectors, which is the whole point. The output is an operator tensor network with one node per party and polynomial links between parties that shared a gate. A column of it is a ket network with the same links, close to the ground vector after normalization.

The theorem and its proof are not geometric and we do not change a word of them; we restate the theorem in the form used here and sketch the estimate.

Precisely.

Theorem 11.1 (Density compression, ). Consider a family of distributed constructions indexed by \(L\ge2\) such that: the numbers of parties and gates are uniformly polynomial; the initial vector is a product of normalized private vectors; every gate is a contraction; each non-private gate involves at most \(b\) parties and each party participates in at most \(b\) non-private gates; each non-private gate is admissible with uniformly polynomial bounds; and the intended physical output of each party has uniformly polynomial dimension, everything else being traced out. Let \(\rho\) be the physical output density (positive, trace at most one). For every fixed \(p>0\) there is an operator tensor network \(\sigma\) with one node per party, a bounded number of links at each node, each link joining two parties that participate in a common non-private gate, uniformly polynomial link dimensions, and \[\lVert \sigma-\rho\rVert_1\le L^{-p}.\] The constants depend only on \(p\), \(b\) and the uniform polynomial bounds, not on private dimensions or on the Schmidt ranks of shared vectors.

Sketch. First eliminate shared covectors: a covector \(\langle \eta|\) on a pair of registers is replaced by appending \(m-1\) copies of \(\eta\) and averaging the \(m\) cyclic permutations of the \(m\) pair registers, a contraction \(T_m\) with \(\lVert T_m-|\eta\rangle^{\otimes m}\langle \eta|\rVert=m^{-1/2}\); with a common fixed garbage vector in every branch, a gate with \(K\) monomials, each with at most \(r\) covectors, is approximated in operator norm within \(rm^{-1/2}\sum\lvert c_\xi\rvert\) by a gate with \(Km^r\) monomials using only shared vectors. Choosing \(m\) polynomial makes this error at most \(\epsilon/(8M)\) per gate, \(M\) the number of non-private gates.

Next, in the density calculation each source operator \(X=|\eta\rangle\langle \tilde\eta|\), with Schmidt data \((\lambda_a,u_a,v_a)\) and \((\mu_c,\tilde u_c,\tilde v_c)\), is replaced by \(\widehat X=\frac1k\sum_{j=1}^kU_j\otimes V_j\) with \(U_j=\sum_{a,c}(\lambda_a\mu_c)^{1/4}g^{(j)}_{ac}|u_a\rangle\langle \tilde u_c|\), \(V_j=\sum_{b,d}(\lambda_b\mu_d)^{1/4}\overline{g^{(j)}_{bd}}|v_b\rangle\langle \tilde v_d|\) and independent standard complex Gaussians \(g^{(j)}_{ac}\). Then \(\mathbb E\widehat X=X\), and the centered coefficients of \(\Delta=\widehat X-X\) have diagonal covariance \(k^{-1}\sqrt{\lambda_a\lambda_b\mu_c\mu_d}\) by the Gaussian pairing formula. Expanding the product over all \(N\) slot positions, \(\widehat\rho-\rho'=\sum_{\varnothing\ne S}F_S\) where \(S\) lists the positions using a correction. For \(\lvert S\rvert=s\), the endpoint parties number at most \(2s\) and participate in at most \(2bs\) gates; expand only those gates into monomials and fix the physical basis entries at those parties, at total weighted cost \(Q(L)^s\) with \(Q\) a fixed polynomial. For a fixed choice, the calculation separates into an affected side, holding the corrected endpoints as free inputs, and an exterior, joined only by the exact shared vectors crossing between them, with Schmidt probability matrices \(\tau,\tilde\tau\). The affected contractions give a block operator \(O\) of norm at most one, the corrected coefficients \(z_{il}\) have covariance \(k^{-s}\sqrt{p_i\tilde p_l}\,\delta\delta\), and the exterior input operator is \(Z=\sum_{il}z_{il}\tau^{1/2}O_{li}^{\mathsf T}\tilde\tau^{1/2}\). Schatten–Hölder with exponents \(4,2,4\) and trace-one weights gives \(\lVert Z\rVert_1\le\lVert \sum_{il}z_{il}\tilde\tau^{1/4}O_{li}\tau^{1/4}\rVert_2\), whose expected square is \(k^{-s}\lVert \widetilde R^{1/4}OR^{1/4}\rVert_2^2\le k^{-s}\) with \(R=\mathrm{diag}(p_i)\otimes\tau\), \(\widetilde R=\mathrm{diag}(\tilde p_l)\otimes\tilde\tau\). The exterior maps and the physical matrix unit do not increase the trace norm. Hence \(\mathbb E\lVert F_S\rVert_1\le Q(L)^sk^{-s/2}\) and \(\mathbb E\lVert \widehat\rho-\rho'\rVert_1\le(1+Q(L)k^{-1/2})^N-1\le\epsilon/4\) for a polynomial \(k\). Fix a realization. The link structure: the common monomial label of a gate is shared along a star between its participants, and each slot’s sample index \(j\) is shared between its two endpoints; all private maps, private source preparations and final private traces are contracted inside each party’s node. ◻

Lemma 11.2 (Column extraction, ). Let \(\Omega\) be a unit vector on a finite tensor product and \(\sigma\) an operator with \(\lVert \sigma-|\Omega\rangle\langle \Omega|\rVert_1\le\eta<1\). Then some product-basis column \(v=\sigma|z\rangle\) is nonzero and satisfies \(\min_\theta\lVert v/\lVert v\rVert-\mathrm e^{i\theta}\Omega\rVert\le2\eta\). Fixing a column in an operator tensor network leaves all virtual dimensions unchanged.

Proof. Let \(E=\sigma-|\Omega\rangle\langle \Omega|\) and \(a_z=\langle\Omega|z\rangle\). Then \(\sum_z\lVert E|z\rangle\rVert^2=\lVert E\rVert_2^2\le\eta^2\) and \(\sum_z\lvert a_z\rvert^2=1\), so some \(z\) with \(a_z\ne0\) has \(\lVert E|z\rangle\rVert\le\eta\lvert a_z\rvert\). For it, \(w=v/a_z\) satisfies \(\lVert w-\Omega\rVert\le\eta\), hence \(w\ne0\) and \(\lVert w/\lVert w\rVert-\Omega\rVert\le2\eta\). Fixing the input index at every node is a contraction of physical legs only. ◻

Assembly on the chain

In words. Apply the compression theorem to the sweep. Its parties are the block owners \(P_i\), with physical output the \(q^{\lvert B_i\rvert}\)-dimensional block register, and the remainders \(R_k\), with no physical output. Every link joins two parties that shared a gate, and by construction those are attached to blocks at most two apart. Put the node of \(R_k\) next to block \(k+1\). Then a bond of the chain between two blocks is crossed only by links among the boundedly many parties attached to the four nearest blocks: constant congestion, and the dimension of a block-to-block bond is a bounded product of polynomial link dimensions. Finally, split each block node into one tensor per site by sequential singular value decompositions; the internal bonds are at most \(q^{\lvert B_i\rvert}\) times the crossing dimension, still polynomial. The column-extraction lemma turns the operator network into a ket network first. Small chains are handled by the trivial exact representation.

Precisely.

Proof of Theorem 1.2. Let \(L_0\) be large enough for Proposition 25 and for the inequalities below; for \(2\le L<L_0\) every vector on \(\Lambda\) is an exact matrix product state of bond dimension at most \(q^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\le q^{L_0/2}\), which is absorbed into \(C\). Let \(L\ge L_0\).

By Proposition 10.4(3) and \(\lVert |u\rangle\langle u|-|v\rangle\langle v|\rVert_1\le2\lVert u-v\rVert\) for vectors of norm at most one, the physical output density \(\rho=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{private}}|\Psi\rangle\langle \Psi|\) satisfies \(\lVert \rho-|\Omega\rangle\langle \Omega|\rVert_1\le 52L^{-11}\). The construction satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem 27 with \(b=40\) and physical output dimensions at most \(q^{4t}\le L^{80D_{24}\log q+1}\), so with accuracy \(L^{-5}\) there is an operator network \(\sigma\) on the party graph with \(\lVert \sigma-|\Omega\rangle\langle \Omega|\rVert_1\le L^{-5}+52L^{-11}\le2L^{-5}\), bounded degree, and link dimensions at most \(C_1L^{r}\). By Lemma 28 some column is a nonzero ket network \(v\) with the same links and \(\min_\theta\lVert v/\lVert v\rVert-\mathrm e^{i\theta}\Omega\rVert\le4L^{-5}\le L^{-1}\).

Placement. Attach \(P_i\) to block \(B_i\) and \(R_k\) to block \(B_{k+1}\). Two parties sharing a gate are, by the proof of Proposition 25, among \(\{P_k,P_{k+1},R_k,R_{k+1}\}\) for some \(k\), hence attached to blocks at most two apart. A bond between blocks \(i\) and \(i+1\) is therefore crossed only by links incident to parties attached to blocks \(i-1,\dots,i+2\); there are at most \(8\) such parties, each of degree at most \(5\cdot40\) (at most two star links and three sample links per three-party gate), so at most \(\chi:=1600\) links cross any block bond. Route each link along the chain between its two blocks; group the indices crossing a block bond into one index of dimension at most \((C_1L^r)^{\chi}\). The tensor at block \(i\) is now a map from its left and right block-bond indices to the block’s physical register \(\mathcal H_{B_i}\), of dimension at most \(q^{4t}\). Writing \(B_i=\{x_1<\dots<x_n\}\), perform singular value decompositions successively across \(x_1|x_2\cdots x_n\), \(x_1x_2|x_3\cdots x_n\), …, treating the two block-bond indices as additional legs attached to the first and last site; the resulting single-site tensors have internal bond dimensions at most \(\min\{q^{j}(C_1L^r)^\chi,\ q^{n-j}(C_1L^r)^\chi\}\le q^{2t}(C_1L^r)^{\chi}\) and the block bonds are unchanged. Thus \(v\) is a matrix product state on \(\Lambda\) with open boundary conditions and bond dimension at most \(q^{2t}(C_1L^r)^\chi\le CL^{c}\), with \(c=40D_{24}\log q+r\chi\). ◻

Remark 29 (Order of constants). Fix \(q,R,J,\Delta\). Theorem 1 gives \(C_{\mathrm{int}}\); Lemma 16 at \(p=24\) gives \(C_{24}\), hence \(D_{24}\) and the block scale \(t\); the number of parties, gates and the participation bound \(b=40\) are then fixed; Theorem 11.1 at accuracy \(L^{-5}\) fixes the covector stack length \(m\), the Gaussian sample count \(k\), and the link exponent \(r\); the congestion \(\chi\) is a numerical constant. None of these depends on the Hamiltonian or on \(L\). The protocol uses \(O(L/\log L)\) auxiliary copies of \(\Omega\), all private.

Discussion

In words. What is different from Hastings. Hastings’ area law proves that the half-chain marginal has an entanglement spectrum whose tail is exponentially small beyond rank \(\exp(O(1))\), by combining a Lieb–Robinson bound, a spectral filter that approximately projects onto the ground state, and a clever bound on the approximate factorization of the filtered state. Arad–Kitaev–Landau–Vazirani replace the filter by an approximate ground-state projector built from Chebyshev polynomials with controlled Schmidt rank, and bootstrap. The present proof of Theorem 1 has no dynamics in it at all: the gap is used only to bound the overlap of a variationally tilted or filtered vector with the ground vector, and the entropy is read off from operator norms by Hölder, a three-lines interpolation and the Araki–Lieb inequality. Its dependence on the gap is linear. The matrix product state theorem has two one-dimensional proofs here: the two-line route, which is a direct consequence of the tail bound and the area law and therefore equally free of dynamics, and the protocol route, which uses a Lieb–Robinson bound in one lemma (the reset) but in a way that has no analogue in Hastings’ construction (a two-replica swap undone by a local unitary), and which otherwise consists of Uhlmann-type ownership changes, random Gaussian compression and column extraction.

What one dimension buys. Three things, each used once. A shell around an interval is crossed by \(O(1)\) interactions, so the buffered discount costs a constant and needs a constant-width buffer. No three owners ever meet at a point of a line, so no encodings are needed when ownership changes. And a chain can be swept, so the party graph has bounded cutwidth. Everything else in the source is either unchanged or disappears.

What does not transfer back. The Araki–Lieb closure fails in two dimensions because the buffer is an annulus of unknown entropy; the sweep fails in two dimensions because a region being repainted has a one-dimensional boundary along which a third owner is adjacent, which the edge bands and point treatments of the source are designed to handle; and the two-line route fails because sequential truncation across nested cuts does not produce a network on the lattice. So the two-dimensional papers are not inefficient versions of this note; they solve problems that do not exist on a line.
